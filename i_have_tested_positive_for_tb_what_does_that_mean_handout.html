Latent TB Infection: Your Diagnosis & Your Treatment Plan

Prepared by your family doctor June 23, 2026

What Does a Positive TB Test Mean?

The short answer: You have latent (lat-ent) TB infection — meaning the TB bacteria are in your body but are completely "asleep." You are not sick, and you cannot spread TB to others. However, treatment is recommended to keep those bacteria asleep for good.

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by a germ called Mycobacterium tuberculosis. When someone breathes in TB germs, the immune system (the body's defence system) usually traps them. The germs don't cause illness — they just stay dormant (inactive), sometimes for years or even a lifetime. This is called Latent TB Infection (LTBI).1

Active TB — the kind that makes people cough, lose weight, or feel very ill — is different from LTBI. Your test shows the latent, inactive form.

Why treatment is being recommended for you: Because of your age, diabetes, and kidney disease (CKD/peritoneal dialysis), there is a higher-than-average chance that the dormant bacteria could "wake up" and become active TB in the future. Treatment now dramatically lowers that risk.2,3

Your Treatment Plan: 4 Months of Rifampin

Your doctor is recommending a medicine called rifampin (RIF-am-pin), taken once daily by mouth for 4 months. This regimen — called 4R — is Health Canada–approved and is one of the most effective and best-tolerated LTBI treatments available. Studies show it works just as well as older 9-month treatments but is shorter and causes fewer liver problems.3

Completing 4 months of rifampin gives you approximately 60–80% protection against latent TB ever becoming active TB. That is a meaningful long-term benefit, especially with your health background.2,3

One important side effect you will notice

Rifampin turns body fluids — urine, tears, sweat — a harmless orange-red colour. This is expected and not dangerous, but it will permanently stain soft contact lenses, so switch to glasses during treatment.

Why Some of Your Other Medicines Need Adjusting

Rifampin is a powerful medicine that also affects the way your body processes several other drugs. It "speeds up" a set of liver enzymes (proteins that break down medicines). This means some of your current medicines get broken down faster than usual, so their levels in your blood drop — and they may stop working as well.1

The four medicines affected, and what we will do about each one, are explained below.

Your Current Medicine What Rifampin Does to It Why That Is a Problem The Plan
Atorvastatin (cholesterol pill) Rifampin lowers atorvastatin levels in your blood by about 55%1 — more than half the medicine disappears before it can work Your cholesterol (LDL) could rise, increasing your risk of heart attack or stroke Switch to pravastatin or rosuvastatin — statins that are not affected by rifampin
Amlodipine (blood-pressure pill) Rifampin lowers amlodipine levels by about 30–40%1 Your blood pressure could rise Increase amlodipine dose to the maximum (10 mg → 20 mg daily), OR add a small water pill (diuretic) such as hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg
Olmesartan (blood-pressure / kidney-protection pill) Rifampin lowers olmesartan levels by about 20–30%1 Blood pressure control could worsen; kidneys lose protection Increase olmesartan to maximum dose (40 mg daily) OR add a small diuretic; monitor potassium closely because kidney disease increases the risk of high potassium
Empagliflozin (diabetes pill) Rifampin lowers empagliflozin levels by about 30%4 Blood sugar may not be as well controlled during treatment Keep the same dose for now, but check blood sugar more often — if it rises consistently, a low dose of basal insulin (long-acting insulin) may be added temporarily
Important: All of these medicine adjustments will be made before you take your first rifampin tablet. This way, your blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar stay under control right from the start.

What Happens Next — Step by Step

  1. Switch your cholesterol pill. We will stop atorvastatin and start you on pravastatin (or rosuvastatin). We will check a lipid (cholesterol) panel at the start, at 4 weeks, and at the end of treatment.
  2. Adjust your blood-pressure medicines. Amlodipine will be increased to the highest approved dose. Olmesartan will also be increased to its maximum dose, or a small water pill will be added. We will monitor your blood pressure and your potassium and kidney values monthly.
  3. Continue empagliflozin — but monitor your blood sugar more closely. Check your fasting blood sugar (first thing in the morning before eating) every 3–4 days for the first two weeks after starting rifampin. Bring your log to every visit.
  4. Baseline blood tests. Before your first rifampin dose we will check your liver enzymes (ALT/AST), full blood count (CBC), kidney function, potassium, bicarbonate, fasting blood sugar, and cholesterol.
  5. Start rifampin 600 mg once daily. Take it at the same time each day. It can be taken with or without food, but avoid large fatty meals if you notice stomach upset.
  6. Phone check-in at Day 7. Your care team will call to see how you are tolerating the medicine.
  7. Clinic visit at Week 2 — blood tests repeated, blood pressure and blood sugar reviewed, medicine tolerability checked.
  8. Monthly clinic visits and blood tests for months 1 through 4 — same panel each time: liver enzymes, blood count, kidney function, potassium, blood sugar, cholesterol, and blood pressure.
  9. End of treatment (Month 4). Final blood tests. Your doctor will review whether to restart atorvastatin, reduce amlodipine back to 10 mg, and reduce olmesartan back to 20 mg — based on how your numbers look.
  10. 3-month follow-up after finishing rifampin. One final check to confirm liver enzymes, cholesterol, blood pressure, and blood sugar have all returned to your personal baseline.

Warning Signs — Stop Rifampin and Call Us Right Away

Contact Dr. Whiting's office immediately (or go to the Emergency Department after hours) if you develop any of these symptoms:
  • Yellow skin or yellow whites of the eyes (jaundice)
  • Dark brown or cola-coloured urine (not the orange-red colour from rifampin — that is harmless)
  • Pain or tenderness in the upper right side of your abdomen (where the liver sits)
  • Severe nausea, vomiting, or loss of appetite that stops you from eating
  • Unexplained fever or rash
  • Unusual bleeding or bruising, or feeling much more tired than usual

If any of these appear, stop taking rifampin until you have spoken with your doctor. A different LTBI treatment option exists if rifampin needs to be stopped.7,8

Your Daily & Weekly Home Checklist

  • Take rifampin at the same time every day — daily adherence is essential for the 4-month course to work
  • Check your blood pressure at home at least twice a day; write down the readings; bring the log to every appointment
  • Check your fasting blood sugar every 3–4 days for the first two weeks, then as agreed with your doctor
  • Watch the colour of your urine — orange-red = normal with rifampin; dark brown or cola colour = call the office
  • Do not wear soft contact lenses during treatment (rifampin will permanently stain them)
  • Avoid alcohol during treatment to protect your liver
  • Tell every healthcare provider (including your dialysis team and pharmacist) that you are taking rifampin — it can affect many medicines
  • Keep all scheduled blood tests, including those coordinated around your peritoneal-dialysis appointments
  • Do not stop any medicine or change any dose without speaking to Dr. Whiting first

How You Might Be Feeling

It is completely normal to feel worried, overwhelmed, or even anxious when you learn you need extra medicines, more blood tests, and changes to medications you have relied on for years.

You may wonder: "Am I sicker than I thought?" or "Why does one infection need so many adjustments?" Those are fair questions, and your feelings make complete sense.

Here is what we want you to know: You are not facing active TB. The changes to your medicines are pre-emptive — we are getting ahead of potential problems before they happen, not reacting to a crisis. The goal is to get you safely through four months and then return as close as possible to your regular routine. We are with you every step of the way.

Questions to Ask Your Care Team

Write down any concerns before your appointments. Here are some questions to get you started:

  1. Which statin am I being switched to, and how will we know if my cholesterol stays under control?
  2. How much will my amlodipine dose be increased, and what signs of blood pressure being too high or too low should I watch for at home?
  3. Will olmesartan be increased, and do I need extra monitoring of my potassium levels?
  4. How often should I check my blood sugar, and what is the plan if it rises significantly?
  5. What are the most important side effects of rifampin that would require me to call you right away?
  6. How will the monthly blood tests be scheduled around my peritoneal-dialysis appointments?
  7. If liver problems develop, what alternative treatment options are available for my latent TB?
The bottom line: Treating latent TB now — with the medication changes and monitoring described above — is the safest, most effective way to protect your long-term health. With careful planning and close follow-up, most people complete this 4-month course without serious problems.3,4
