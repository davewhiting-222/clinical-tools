Making Sense of Chronic Fatigue in Adolescents and Young Adults

Prepared by your family doctor
What You Need to Know: Persistent fatigue in teens and young adults can have multiple causes that often overlap. Understanding these causes helps us create the right treatment plan for you.

Chronic fatigue is more than just feeling tired after a busy day. It's a persistent exhaustion that doesn't improve with rest and can significantly impact your daily life, school performance, and relationships. Let's explore the most common causes in your age group and what we can do about them.

Understanding the Main Causes

Condition Key Features What It Means for You
Residual Fatigue from Depression • Fatigue persists even when mood improves
• History of depression since childhood
• Sleep feels unrefreshing
• Low energy despite treatment 		Depression can change brain chemistry and sleep patterns in ways that cause lasting fatigue, even after mood symptoms improve.
Chronic Fatigue Syndrome/ME • Fatigue for 6+ months
• Gets worse after physical or mental activity
• Sleep doesn't restore energy
• Difficulty concentrating 		A complex condition where your body's energy systems don't work properly. Often develops after infections or stress.
Sleep Disorder-Related Fatigue • Takes 1-2 hours to fall asleep
• Staying up late (midnight or later)
• Waking early but hard to get up
• Screen time affecting sleep 		Your internal clock may be disrupted, making it hard to get quality sleep even when you spend enough time in bed.
Vitamin D Deficiency • Very common in teens and young adults
• Can cause fatigue and mood changes
• Often occurs with limited sun exposure 		Low vitamin D affects energy production at the cellular level and can worsen mood symptoms.
Autism-Related Executive Dysfunction • Sensory overload drains energy
• Routine changes cause stress
• Social interactions require extra effort
• Brain works harder to process daily life 		Your brain may use more energy than typical to navigate daily situations, leading to chronic exhaustion.

Your Action Plan

Sleep Hygiene - Start Here First

  • Set a consistent bedtime and wake time (even on weekends)
  • Stop all screens 1 hour before bed
  • Keep bedroom cool (18-20°C), dark, and quiet
  • Try melatonin 0.5-3mg, 30 minutes before desired bedtime
  • Get morning sunlight exposure within 1 hour of waking
Vitamin D Supplementation: Since we can't easily test vitamin D levels in BC, it's safe and recommended to take 1000-2000 IU daily, especially during fall and winter months.

Managing Post-Exertional Malaise (if you have CFS/ME)

Important: If your fatigue gets significantly worse after physical or mental activity, you may need to pace your activities carefully. This means staying within your energy limits rather than pushing through fatigue.

Pacing strategies include:

  • Breaking large tasks into smaller chunks
  • Planning rest periods between activities
  • Learning to say no when your energy is low
  • Keeping an energy diary to identify your limits

For Autism-Related Fatigue

Energy Conservation: Your brain works harder than most to process daily life. This isn't weakness - it's just how your neurology works. Planning for this can help preserve your energy.

Helpful strategies:

  • Create predictable routines where possible
  • Use noise-cancelling headphones in busy environments
  • Schedule downtime after social activities
  • Identify your sensory triggers and plan around them

When to Follow Up

See your doctor if:
  • Your fatigue is getting worse despite these strategies
  • You develop new symptoms like chest pain or dizziness
  • Your mood is declining significantly
  • You're missing school or work regularly due to fatigue

Supporting Your Recovery

Recovery from chronic fatigue takes time and patience. Here's what helps:

  1. Start small: Pick one or two strategies to focus on first
  2. Track your progress: Keep a simple diary of energy levels and sleep
  3. Be patient: Improvements often take weeks to months
  4. Build your support network: Tell family and friends what you're dealing with
  5. Work with your healthcare team: Regular check-ins help adjust your plan
Remember: Chronic fatigue is a real medical condition, not laziness or a character flaw. With the right approach, most people see significant improvement in their energy levels and quality of life.
