Comprehensive Skin Examination

What to Expect — and How to Tell Us Your Preferences

You Are in Control

A skin examination is a collaborative process. Nothing will be done without your knowledge and consent, and you may stop or modify the examination at any time, for any reason. This handout explains what the exam involves and gives you a simple way to tell us your preferences before we begin.

Who Will Be Examining You?

Your examination will be performed by a family physician with approximately 40 years of clinical experience. In addition to broad general practice training, your physician has pursued specialized, focused professional development in skin cancer detection and dermoscopy — the technique of examining skin lesions with a magnifying instrument that illuminates the deeper layers of the skin invisible to the naked eye.

This advanced training has been completed through HealthCert, a leading professional development organization for skin cancer medicine based in Australia, whose programs are quality-assured by Bond University and accredited by the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP). HealthCert's curriculum spans skin cancer medicine, dermoscopy, and skin cancer surgery, and is recognized internationally.

Why Was a Skin Exam Recommended for You?

A full skin examination is generally recommended for patients who have a higher-than-average risk of developing skin cancer. Risk factors that may have prompted this referral include one or more of the following:

  • A family history of skin cancer, particularly melanoma
  • A personal history of a previous skin cancer of any type
  • A higher number of moles (nevi) than average, or the presence of unusual-looking moles (sometimes called atypical or dysplastic nevi)
  • Certain physical traits associated with increased risk, such as fair skin, light eyes, or a tendency to burn rather than tan
  • An underlying genetic or immune condition that predisposes to skin cancer

Being referred for this exam is a proactive step — it means your healthcare team is being thorough on your behalf.

The Purpose of the Examination

The goal of the exam is straightforward: to find lesions that might be concerning before they cause problems. Skin cancers, including melanoma, often begin in areas that are difficult or impossible to see without assistance — the back, behind the ears, the soles of the feet, or beneath the hairline. They can also be easy to dismiss as a harmless freckle, age spot, or benign mole.

Dermoscopy allows your physician to examine the structure beneath the surface of a lesion — its blood vessel patterns, pigment distribution, and internal architecture — providing far more information than a visual inspection alone. When a lesion looks borderline, dermoscopy often resolves the uncertainty without the need for a biopsy.

An Honest Word About Limitations

No examination technique — including dermoscopy by an experienced physician — is perfect. Benign lesions can occasionally look suspicious, and rarely, a skin cancer can appear almost entirely normal. This is why your ongoing vigilance between appointments is essential. Please bring any new, changing, bleeding, or non-healing spot to our attention promptly, rather than waiting for your next scheduled exam. When in doubt, come in.

What the Examination Involves

The examination is a systematic head-to-toe inspection of the skin. Efficiency and thoroughness go hand in hand — your physician will work through areas of the body in an organized sequence, uncovering only the specific area being examined at any given moment before moving on. The full exam typically takes 20 to 30 minutes.

Areas Routinely Examined

The examination covers the face, scalp surface (where accessible), neck, ears, chest, abdomen, back, arms, hands, legs, and feet — including the soles and between the toes, which are often overlooked but are sites where melanomas can occur.

Areas Not Routinely Examined

Two areas are excluded from the routine examination unless you raise a specific clinical concern about them:

  • The hair-bearing scalp: Dense hair makes accurate skin inspection of the scalp unreliable without specialized equipment. If you have a specific spot on your scalp you are concerned about, please mention it.
  • The genitalia: This area is not included in the routine skin cancer examination.

Clothing and Sensitive Areas

We understand that undressing for a medical examination requires trust, and we take that seriously. Here is what the examination involves and what options are available to you.

Upper Body

We will ask you to remove all clothing from the upper half of your body, including your bra if applicable. This allows an unobstructed view of the chest, abdomen, upper back, and shoulders. A gown will be provided, and only the area actively being examined will be exposed at any time.

Lower Body and Underwear

You are entirely welcome to leave your lower underwear on throughout the examination. However, it is worth understanding why complete coverage of this region matters clinically.

Why the Groin and Lower Back Area Are Clinically Important

Melanoma can occur anywhere on the body — including the lower back, buttocks, inner thighs, and groin. These locations are particularly prone to being missed because patients cannot easily see them, and they are often covered by clothing. A skin cancer in these areas is no less serious than one elsewhere; the difference is that it may go undetected for longer. If you are comfortable allowing these areas to be examined, the clinical benefit is real.

If you choose to keep your lower underwear on, you have two options, and we will ask you to indicate your preference before the examination begins:

  • Option A: You give permission for your physician to gently move the waistband and edges of your underwear as needed to view the skin of the lower back, buttocks, lower abdomen, and groin — without removing the garment.
  • Option B: You prefer these areas to remain completely untouched and unexamined.

Either choice is fully respected. There is no pressure and no judgement.

Chaperone Policy

All female patients are offered a chaperone — a trained member of our clinical staff who will be present throughout the examination. You may also request a chaperone regardless of your gender. Please indicate your preference on the form below.

Your Ongoing Role Between Appointments

The most effective skin cancer surveillance happens between clinic visits. Get to know your own skin. The "ABCDE" signs to watch for in any mole or spot are: Asymmetry, irregular Border, uneven Colour, a Diameter larger than 6 mm, or any Evolving change in size, shape, colour, or behaviour (itching, bleeding, crusting). Any spot that is new, changing, or simply worrying you is worth showing us — please don't wait.
