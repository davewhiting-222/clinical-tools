Blood Thinners and Minor Surgery: When to Stop Your Medications

Prepared by your family doctor
December 2024
Important: Never stop or change your blood thinner medications without first speaking to your doctor. This handout provides general guidance, but your specific situation may require different timing.

Understanding Blood Thinners

Blood thinners (also called anticoagulants and antiplatelet medications) reduce your risk of blood clots and strokes. However, they also increase your risk of bleeding during surgical procedures. The key is finding the right balance between preventing clots and reducing bleeding risk.

Two Main Types of Blood Thinners

Anticoagulants: These prevent blood clots from forming. Examples include warfarin and newer medications called NOACs (Novel Oral Anticoagulants).

Antiplatelet agents: These prevent platelets (blood cells) from sticking together. Examples include ASA (aspirin) and clopidogrel (Plavix).

Factors That Affect Timing

Your Individual Risk

  • Kidney function: If your kidneys don't work as well, medications may stay in your system longer
  • Age: Older adults may need longer time to clear medications
  • Other medications: Some drugs can affect how blood thinners work
  • Your bleeding risk: Some people are naturally at higher risk for bleeding

Type of Procedure

Lower bleeding risk procedures (may require shorter stops or no interruption):

  • Dental cleaning or simple tooth extraction
  • Skin biopsy or mole removal
  • Cataract surgery
  • Endoscopy without biopsy

Higher bleeding risk procedures (typically require stopping blood thinners):

  • Joint injections
  • Colonoscopy with polyp removal
  • Most surgeries, even minor ones
  • Procedures near important organs or blood vessels

Your Action Plan

Before Any Procedure:

  1. Tell all healthcare providers about your blood thinner medications
  2. Ask specifically if you should stop your medication and when
  3. Get clear instructions about when to restart after the procedure
  4. Ask about bridging (temporary alternative blood thinner) if needed

Questions to Ask Your Healthcare Team

  • Should I stop my blood thinner for this procedure?
  • If yes, how many days before should I stop?
  • When can I restart my medication after the procedure?
  • Do I need any blood tests before the procedure?
  • Should I use a different blood thinner temporarily (bridging)?
  • What signs of bleeding should I watch for after the procedure?

Special Considerations

Warfarin (Coumadin) Users

If you take warfarin, you may need blood tests (INR) before your procedure to make sure your blood clotting has returned to a safe level. Your doctor may also recommend "bridging" with heparin injections if you're at high risk for blood clots.

Emergency Procedures

If you need emergency surgery, tell the medical team immediately about your blood thinner medications. They have ways to reverse the effects quickly if necessary.

After Your Procedure

  • Follow your doctor's instructions about when to restart your blood thinner
  • Watch for signs of unusual bleeding (heavy bruising, prolonged bleeding from cuts, blood in urine or stool)
  • Contact your doctor if you have concerns about bleeding or clotting
Never stop your blood thinner on your own. The risk of stroke or blood clots from stopping medication incorrectly can be life-threatening. Always work with your healthcare team to make these decisions safely.
