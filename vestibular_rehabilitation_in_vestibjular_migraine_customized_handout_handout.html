Vestibular Rehabilitation & Vestibular Migraine

Prepared by your family doctor July 30, 2026

What Is Vestibular Migraine?

Vestibular migraine (VM) is a type of migraine where dizziness, spinning sensations (vertigo), and balance problems are the main symptoms — sometimes even without a headache. It is one of the most common causes of recurrent dizziness in adults and can significantly affect your day-to-day life.1

Between attacks, many people develop lasting sensitivity to movement and visual motion — sometimes called Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness (PPPD) (a mouthful, but it simply means your brain stays "on edge" about balance signals even when an acute attack is not happening). This is where vestibular rehabilitation can play an important role.2

🧠 Key Concept Vestibular migraine has two phases that matter for treatment: the acute attack (the flare) and the interictal period (the time in between attacks). Treatment — including rehabilitation — works differently in each phase.

What Is Vestibular Rehabilitation?

Vestibular rehabilitation therapy (VRT) is a specialised physiotherapy program designed to help your brain recalibrate how it processes balance signals from your eyes, inner ears, and muscles. It typically involves:3

  • Gaze stabilisation exercises (training your eyes and inner ears to work together)
  • Habituation exercises (gradually exposing you to the movements or environments that provoke dizziness, to reduce sensitivity over time)
  • Balance retraining and gait (walking) exercises
  • Graded return to daily and physical activities
💡 Think of it this way Your balance system is like a set of weighing scales that has gotten out of calibration. VRT is the process of slowly and safely recalibrating those scales. It takes patience, but the brain is remarkably good at adapting.

What Does the Evidence Say for Vestibular Migraine?

This is an area where it is important to be honest with you. The evidence for VRT in vestibular migraine is more limited than you might expect, and the benefit depends greatly on what specifically is causing your ongoing symptoms.4

⚠️ Important Nuance While vestibular rehabilitation is frequently recommended — including by neurologists — high-quality clinical trials specifically in vestibular migraine are still limited. The benefit varies considerably depending on which part of your condition is being treated.4,5

Where VRT Is Most Likely to Help You

Research and clinical experience point to two situations where VRT provides the most meaningful benefit:5,6

✅ Strongest Evidence: Between-Attack (Interictal) Symptoms Many people with vestibular migraine develop motion sensitivity, visual vertigo, or PPPD between acute attacks — a kind of "background dizziness" driven by the brain being over-sensitive. VRT is highly effective at resetting this central sensory integration (how your brain weighs up the signals from your eyes, inner ears, and body). If this describes your current experience, you are the best candidate for VRT.2,5
✅ Best Results: When Used Alongside Preventive Medication Studies suggest that VRT works much better once your acute attack frequency is at least partially controlled with preventive (prophylactic) medications — such as beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol), topiramate, or newer CGRP inhibitors. Trying to do VRT while still having frequent acute attacks can actually worsen sensitisation.5,7

Where VRT Is Less Helpful

🔴 Less Benefit: During Active, Poorly Controlled Attacks If acute vestibular migraine attacks remain frequent and severe, VRT exercises can temporarily increase symptoms and may not produce lasting improvement. Getting your acute attack frequency under better control first is a priority.4

Your Referral Options in Kelowna

We have discussed the options for getting you access to vestibular rehabilitation, and I want to lay these out clearly so you can make an informed decision. There is no perfect path here — each option has real trade-offs.

Option 1: Private Vestibular Physiotherapy Recommended Starting Point

You can self-refer (or I can provide a letter of referral) to a private physiotherapy clinic in Kelowna with a vestibular-trained therapist. Several clinics in the Okanagan offer this service. This is the most direct and fastest route to care.

  • Advantage: You can typically be seen within weeks, not months. You choose the timing and pace.
  • Disadvantage: There is an out-of-pocket cost (sessions typically $100–$160 each). Extended health benefits through your employer or MSP supplementary coverage may help offset this — worth checking your plan.
  • What to look for: Ask specifically for a physiotherapist with vestibular rehabilitation training or certification (CVESP or equivalent).
Option 2: Referral to KGH Neurodiagnostics Long Wait — Uncertain Outcome

Kelowna General Hospital has a Neurodiagnostics Department that can be involved in complex dizziness assessments. However, this pathway has two significant challenges:

  • Wait times are typically very long — often many months.
  • Depending on the referral criteria at the time, they may not accept a referral from a family physician for this indication without a specialist's request.
  • This route is more useful for diagnostic clarification than for delivering ongoing vestibular rehabilitation.
Option 3: Referral to ENT → Onward to a Specialised Centre in Vancouver Uncertain — May Be Declined

A referral to the Ear, Nose & Throat (ENT) service could, in principle, lead to onward referral to a specialised vestibular centre in Vancouver (such as those associated with Vancouver General Hospital or St. Paul's). However, there is a complication:

  • The ENT team has already indicated that there is not much more they can offer at their level for your condition. They may decline to see you again, or may not provide an onward referral.
  • If accepted, wait times to a Vancouver specialist centre can be substantial.
  • This path may be worth pursuing if your symptoms escalate or if private physiotherapy does not achieve adequate benefit — it keeps a specialist pathway open.
📋 Dr. Whiting's Suggestion Given the evidence and your specific situation, a reasonable approach would be to:
  1. Ensure your preventive medication is optimised first (to reduce acute attack frequency).
  2. Then pursue private vestibular physiotherapy as the fastest, most direct route — particularly targeted at your between-attack motion sensitivity.
  3. Keep the ENT/Vancouver specialist option in reserve if private physiotherapy does not provide sufficient benefit.

What to Expect If You Start Vestibular Rehabilitation

If you decide to pursue VRT, here is a realistic picture of what the process involves:

Phase Typical Timing What Happens
Assessment Session 1 Detailed evaluation of your balance, eye movements, gait, and specific triggers. The therapist designs a personalised program.
Early exercises Weeks 1–3 Gentle habituation and gaze exercises. Temporary symptom increase is normal and expected — this is the brain beginning to recalibrate.
Progression Weeks 4–8 Exercises become more challenging. Balance and daily function typically begin to improve.
Consolidation Months 2–4 Home exercise program continues. Sessions taper. Most meaningful gains occur in this window.
⚠️ A Common Misconception Many people stop VRT early because exercises temporarily make them feel worse. This initial worsening is actually a sign the therapy is working — your brain is being challenged to change. Always discuss symptom changes with your physiotherapist before stopping.

Supporting Your Recovery — What Else Can Help

VRT works best alongside the foundational lifestyle strategies that reduce migraine burden overall:8

  • Sleep regularity: Go to bed and wake at consistent times — sleep disruption is one of the strongest migraine triggers.
  • Hydration: Aim for 8–10 cups of fluid daily. Dehydration sensitises the vestibular system.
  • Dietary triggers: Common VM triggers include caffeine, alcohol (particularly red wine), aged cheeses, and MSG. A food diary can help identify your personal triggers.
  • Stress management: Stress amplifies both migraine frequency and vestibular sensitivity. Even brief daily relaxation practice (e.g., slow breathing, short walks) helps.
  • Gradual re-engagement with activity: Avoiding all movement due to fear of triggering dizziness can worsen PPPD over time. Graduated activity — guided by your physiotherapist — is key.
🌱 A Note on the Journey Living with vestibular migraine is genuinely difficult — the unpredictability of attacks and background dizziness takes a real toll. Your experience is valid. Recovery with VRT tends to be gradual, but many people achieve meaningful improvement in their daily function and confidence. You are not dealing with this alone.
