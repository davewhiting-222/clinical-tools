Understanding Sugar Substitutes

Prepared by your family doctor
December 2024
Key Point: Sugar substitutes can help reduce calories short-term, but long-term use may carry unexpected health risks. The goal should be gradually reducing your overall preference for sweet tastes.

Many Canadians use sugar substitutes (also called "sweeteners") to cut calories or manage blood sugar. While they can be helpful tools, new research shows they're not just "empty" ingredients—they can affect your brain, gut, and even your heart.

The Short-Term vs. Long-Term Reality

Replacing sugary drinks with diet versions can help you lose a small amount of weight (about 0.7 kg or 1.5 lbs) over a few months.1 However, people who use them for many years actually have a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes and heart disease.1,2 They may also keep your "sweet tooth" active, making it harder to enjoy naturally unsweetened foods.1

Common Types of Sweeteners

Type Examples Key Features
Artificial (Synthetic) Aspartame (Equal), Sucralose (Splenda) Zero calories, much sweeter than sugar
Natural High-Intensity Stevia, Monk Fruit Plant-based, generally don't raise blood sugar
Sugar Alcohols (Polyols) Erythritol, Xylitol, Sorbitol Fewer calories than sugar, common in "keto" products

Important Heart Health Concerns

Red Flag Alert: Recent studies have raised serious concerns about two common sugar alcohols: Erythritol and Xylitol.

Blood Clot Risk

  • High levels of these sweeteners in blood have been linked to increased risk of heart attack and stroke8,9,10
  • Research shows they can make blood platelets more "sticky," potentially causing dangerous clots11,12,13
  • If you have heart disease or diabetes, be extra cautious with products containing these ingredients9,11

Brain Confusion and Cravings

The Brain-Sweetener Disconnect: When you taste something sweet but your body doesn't get the expected calories, your brain's hunger center may become more active, leading to increased cravings later.14

Scientists have found that some sweeteners, like Sucralose, might "trick" your brain. This mismatch between sweet taste and missing calories can lead to stronger cravings for sweet foods, especially in women and people who are overweight.14

Digestive Side Effects

Sugar alcohols are known for causing stomach issues because your body doesn't fully absorb them:

  • Common symptoms: Gas, bloating, and diarrhea7,15
  • Best tolerated: Erythritol usually causes fewer problems7
  • Most problematic: Maltitol and Sorbitol are more likely to cause digestive distress7,16
  • Headaches: About 2% of people may find Aspartame triggers headaches or migraines17,18

What to Know in Canada

Canadian Regulations: Allulose, popular in the U.S., is not yet approved as a food ingredient in Canada. You may only find it in certain health supplements.19,20

Cost consideration: "Natural" sweeteners like Monk Fruit and Stevia are often the most expensive options at Canadian retailers.21,22

Critical Pet Safety Warning

DANGER TO DOGS: Xylitol is deadly to dogs. Even small amounts found in sugar-free gum, candy, or peanut butter can cause dangerous blood sugar drops or liver failure.23,24,25 Always keep sugar-free products safely away from pets.

Practical Action Steps

Your Sweetener Strategy Checklist:

  • ☐ Read ingredient labels carefully—look for erythritol and xylitol if you have heart concerns
  • ☐ Start reducing overall sweetness gradually rather than relying on substitutes long-term
  • ☐ Choose water, unsweetened tea, or sparkling water with lemon as your go-to drinks
  • ☐ If using sweeteners, treat them as temporary tools while transitioning to less sweet foods
  • ☐ Keep all sugar-free products away from pets
  • ☐ Monitor your body's response—stop if you experience headaches or digestive issues

The Bottom Line

The healthiest choice for your body is water or unsweetened beverages. If you use sugar substitutes, think of them as a bridge to help you gradually move away from high-sugar foods rather than a permanent solution. Your taste buds can adapt to enjoy less sweetness over time—it just takes patience and consistency.
