Pityriasis Rosea

Prepared by your family doctor July 6, 2026

What Is Pityriasis Rosea?

Pityriasis rosea (say: pit-ih-RY-ah-sis ROH-zee-ah) is a common, self-limiting (goes away on its own) skin rash. It usually affects the trunk (chest, back, and belly), neck, and upper arms and legs. It is not contagious — you cannot catch it from another person, and you cannot pass it on.

ⓘ The Good News
In the vast majority of cases, pityriasis rosea clears up completely on its own within 6 to 12 weeks — with no scarring and no long-term effects on your health.

What Causes It?

Researchers believe pityriasis rosea is triggered by a reactivation (waking back up) of a common human herpes virus — specifically Human Herpesvirus 6 or 7 (HHV-6/HHV-7). These are not the same viruses that cause cold sores or genital herpes. Almost all adults carry these viruses silently without knowing it.

The rash is not caused by a fungal infection, an allergy, or anything you ate or did. It can occur at any age but is most common in people between 10 and 35 years old. It can occasionally be triggered by certain medications, so it is worth mentioning to your doctor all the medications you are currently taking.

⚠ A Note for Pregnant Patients
If you are pregnant and develop this rash, please let your doctor know right away. There is some evidence that pityriasis rosea in early pregnancy may be associated with a small risk of complications. Closer monitoring may be recommended.

What Does It Look Like?

Stage 1 — The Herald Patch

The rash usually starts with a single, larger oval or round patch called the "herald patch" (sometimes called the "mother patch"). It is typically salmon-pink in colour, 2–10 cm across, with a scaly, slightly raised border. It often appears on the chest, back, or belly. Many people mistake it for ringworm (a fungal infection) at first.

Stage 2 — The General Rash

Within 1 to 2 weeks, smaller patches spread across the trunk and upper limbs. These patches often follow the lines of the ribs, creating a pattern that looks like a drooping "Christmas tree" on the back. The face, scalp, palms, and soles are usually not affected.

💡 What to Expect
The rash may look alarming at first, especially when it spreads in week one or two. This spreading is normal and expected — it does not mean the condition is getting worse or becoming dangerous.

Natural History — How It Unfolds Over Time

Timeframe What Typically Happens
Week 1–2 Herald patch appears. You may have mild flu-like feelings (fatigue, headache, sore throat) — this is less common but does occur.
Week 2–4 Smaller patches spread across the trunk and upper limbs. Itching (pruritis) is often at its worst during this phase.
Week 4–8 Most patches begin to fade from the centre outward. Itching usually improves.
Week 6–12 Rash is fully resolved in most people. Some darker-skinned individuals may notice temporary darkening of the skin (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation) that gradually fades.
Beyond 12 weeks Rarely, the rash can last longer (up to 5 months). If this happens, let your doctor know for reassessment.
✓ Recurrence Is Uncommon
Once you have had pityriasis rosea, your immune system builds a lasting response. Most people only ever have it once in their lifetime. Recurrence is estimated to occur in fewer than 3% of people.

Managing Itch (Pruritis)

Not everyone with pityriasis rosea is bothered by itch — but for those who are, it can range from mild to quite uncomfortable. The following strategies are evidence-based and recommended by dermatologists (skin specialists).

First-Line: Soothing the Skin

  • Cool or lukewarm showers — hot water worsens itch. Keep showers short.
  • Fragrance-free moisturising creams (e.g., Cerave®, Aveeno® Skin Relief, Glaxal Base®) applied immediately after patting skin dry.
  • Loose, breathable clothing (cotton) to reduce friction and heat on affected skin.
  • Cool compresses applied to the itchiest areas for temporary relief.

Second-Line: Topical (On the Skin) Treatments

  • Topical corticosteroids (steroid cream) — A mild-to-moderate strength steroid cream such as hydrocortisone 1% (available over the counter at Canadian pharmacies) or a prescription-strength cream (e.g., betamethasone valerate) can reduce inflammation and itch. Use only as directed — do not apply to the face or skin folds unless specifically told to.
  • Calamine lotion — An inexpensive, widely available option for soothing itchy skin.
  • Menthol-containing creams — Can provide a cooling, anti-itch sensation.

Third-Line: Oral (Taken by Mouth) Medications for Significant Itch

  • Antihistamines — Cetirizine (Reactine®) 10 mg once daily or loratadine (Claritin®) 10 mg once daily are non-drowsy options available over the counter. For nighttime itch disrupting sleep, diphenhydramine (Benadryl®) or hydroxyzine (prescription) may be helpful due to their sedating effect.
⚠ What About Acyclovir?
Some studies suggest the antiviral medication acyclovir (used to treat herpes viruses) may shorten the duration of the rash and reduce itch when started early. However, evidence is mixed and current Canadian dermatology guidelines do not routinely recommend it. Your doctor will discuss whether it is appropriate for you based on your specific situation.

Sunlight and UV Therapy

Some evidence suggests that natural sunlight (with appropriate sun safety) or supervised UV light therapy (phototherapy) may speed up resolution of the rash. However, the evidence quality is moderate. Discuss this with your doctor before deliberately increasing sun exposure, especially if you are on photosensitising medications.

Your Action Plan

Use this checklist to manage your condition at home and know when to follow up:

  • Switch to short, cool or lukewarm showers and avoid hot baths.
  • Apply a fragrance-free moisturiser (e.g., Cerave®, Glaxal Base®) at least once daily, or after every shower.
  • Wear loose, breathable cotton clothing to avoid irritating the rash.
  • Try a non-drowsy antihistamine (cetirizine or loratadine) from your pharmacy if itch is bothering you during the day.
  • Use a mild steroid cream (hydrocortisone 1%) on itchy patches if needed — ask your pharmacist for guidance.
  • Avoid scratching — it can break the skin and lead to infection.
  • Keep a simple diary of how the rash is changing week to week.
  • Return to see Dr. Whiting if the rash has not improved after 10 weeks, is spreading to your face or palms and soles, is causing severe itch interfering with sleep, or if you develop a fever or the rash looks infected (swollen, warm, pus).
🚨 When to Seek Medical Attention Sooner
  • You are pregnant or think you may be pregnant.
  • The rash is very widespread and itch is severe or not responding to over-the-counter treatments.
  • The rash does not follow the typical pattern described above — unusual locations (face, palms, soles) or blisters are present.
  • You have a weakened immune system (e.g., from medication or illness).
  • The skin appears infected (red streaks, increasing swelling, pus, fever).

Quick Summary

ⓘ Key Takeaways
  • Pityriasis rosea is a common, self-limiting rash likely triggered by a reactivated herpes virus (not the kind that causes cold sores or genital herpes).
  • It is NOT contagious, NOT caused by fungi, and NOT due to something you did wrong.
  • It resolves completely on its own in 6–12 weeks in most people.
  • Treatment focuses on keeping the skin comfortable and relieving itch — moisturisers, steroid creams, and antihistamines are the main tools.
  • Contact your doctor if you are pregnant, or if the rash is atypical, severe, or persists beyond 10–12 weeks.
