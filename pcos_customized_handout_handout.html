Understanding PCOS: Your Personalised Guide

Prepared by your family doctor July 14, 2026

What Is PCOS?

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common hormonal conditions affecting women and people with ovaries — it affects roughly 1 in 10 in Canada. Polycystic means "many cysts," referring to small fluid-filled sacs that can develop on the ovaries. Despite the name, you do not have to have ovarian cysts to have PCOS.

PCOS is diagnosed when at least two of the following three features are present:

  • Irregular or infrequent periods (your ovaries are not releasing eggs regularly)
  • Signs of excess androgens (male-type hormones) — such as acne, excess body or facial hair, or hair thinning on the scalp
  • Polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound
🔑 Important to know PCOS is not just a reproductive condition — it affects your metabolism, energy levels, mood, skin, and long-term health. Understanding your version of PCOS is the first step toward feeling better.

In your case, three key features are particularly important: marked hyperandrogenism (higher-than-normal male hormones), severe insulin resistance (your body's cells are not responding well to insulin), and elevated CRP (a marker of low-grade inflammation throughout the body). Each of these influences the others — and together they can make symptoms more pronounced. The good news: all three respond well to the right treatment plan.

How PCOS Can Affect You Day-to-Day

Because PCOS touches so many body systems, its effects can be wide-ranging:

Body System Common Effects
Hormones & Periods Irregular, infrequent, or absent periods; difficulty becoming pregnant; anovulation (no egg release)
Skin & Hair Acne (especially jawline/chin), excess facial or body hair (hirsutism), scalp hair thinning, darkened skin patches (acanthosis nigricans)
Metabolism Weight gain (especially around the middle), difficulty losing weight, blood sugar fluctuations, increased risk of type 2 diabetes

Cardiovascular Higher risk of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and heart disease over time
Inflammation Elevated CRP (C-reactive protein) reflects ongoing low-grade inflammation that can worsen all other systems

1. Insulin Resistance — The Central Driver

Insulin is a hormone made by your pancreas that acts like a "key" to let sugar (glucose) into your cells for energy. With insulin resistance, the cells do not respond well to that key. Your pancreas compensates by making more insulin. Those high insulin levels then signal your ovaries to produce excess testosterone and other androgens — fuelling the hormonal side of PCOS.

⚠️ Why this matters for you Severe insulin resistance means your pancreas is working very hard. Over years, this increases the risk of developing pre-diabetes and type 2 diabetes. Getting on top of insulin resistance now is one of the most important things you can do for your long-term health.

2. Hyperandrogenism — The Hormonal Amplifier

High androgens (testosterone and related hormones) make the ovaries produce even more androgens — a kind of self-amplifying loop. High androgens directly disrupt the normal development of egg follicles in the ovaries, contributing to irregular periods. They also worsen the body's sensitivity to insulin (making insulin resistance worse) and promote the storage of fat around the abdomen, which in turn drives more inflammation.

For you, marked hyperandrogenism explains many of the more visible symptoms such as acne, excess hair, and skin changes. These are not cosmetic nuisances — they are windows into what is happening metabolically.

3. Chronic Inflammation — The Hidden Fuel

CRP (C-reactive protein) is a protein your liver releases in response to inflammation anywhere in the body. Elevated CRP in PCOS suggests there is ongoing, low-grade inflammation that is not caused by infection — it is driven by the metabolic dysfunction itself. This inflammation:

  • Worsens insulin resistance directly (inflammatory molecules interfere with insulin signalling)
  • Stimulates the ovaries and adrenal glands (a small gland near the kidney) to produce more androgens
  • Damages the lining of blood vessels over time, contributing to cardiovascular risk
  • Contributes to fatigue, brain fog, and mood changes
💡 The empowering flip side Because these three systems reinforce each other, breaking the cycle anywhere creates a positive chain reaction. Improving insulin resistance lowers androgens AND reduces inflammation. Lower inflammation improves insulin sensitivity. Every positive step you take ripples through the entire system.

Your Treatment Options

There is no single "cure" for PCOS, but with the right combination of approaches, symptoms can improve significantly — and so can your long-term health. Here is an overview of the options relevant to your situation.

🩺 The Birth Control Pill (Combined Oral Contraceptive)

A combined oral contraceptive pill (OCP) containing both estrogen and progestin (a synthetic progesterone) is often the first hormonal treatment for PCOS. For PCOS management, its goal is not primarily contraception — it is hormonal regulation.

What it doesWhy it helps
Reduces LH (a hormone that drives androgen production)Lowers testosterone levels made by the ovaries
Increases SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin) in the bloodBinds up free testosterone, making less available to cause symptoms
Regulates the uterine liningProvides regular withdrawal bleeds, protecting the uterus from abnormal cell growth
Reduces androgen-driven acne and hirsutismSkin and hair improvements typically noticed within 3–6 months
🔑 Important note Some oral contraceptive pills can slightly worsen insulin resistance. Your doctor will choose a formulation with a progestin that has a more favourable metabolic profile. This is especially important given your severe insulin resistance.

🌿 Spironolactone — Targeting Androgens Directly

Spironolactone (brand name Aldactone® in Canada) is a medication originally developed for blood pressure, but at lower doses it is a powerful anti-androgen (a drug that blocks the effects of male hormones). It works in two ways:

  • Blocks androgen receptors — testosterone cannot dock onto cells and cause symptoms
  • Reduces androgen production in the ovaries and adrenal glands

This is particularly effective for acne, excess facial and body hair, and scalp hair thinning. It is typically used alongside the birth control pill, as it can cause irregular periods on its own, and must be avoided during pregnancy.

⚠️ Pregnancy precaution Spironolactone must not be taken during pregnancy, as it can affect fetal development. Reliable contraception (such as the OCP) is required while taking it.

💊 Metformin — Tackling Insulin Resistance

Metformin (brand names Glucophage®, Glumetza® in Canada) is a medication most people associate with type 2 diabetes — but it is also a cornerstone of PCOS treatment, especially when insulin resistance is prominent. It works by making your body's cells more responsive to insulin (improving insulin sensitivity) and reducing the amount of glucose your liver releases into the bloodstream.

In PCOS, metformin can:

  • Lower circulating insulin levels, which reduces the signal to produce excess androgens
  • Help restore more regular ovulation and menstrual cycles
  • Support modest weight loss and reduce abdominal fat
  • Lower CRP and other inflammatory markers over time
  • Significantly reduce the long-term risk of developing type 2 diabetes

It is started at a low dose and gradually increased to minimise digestive side effects (nausea, loose stools). Taking it with food and using the extended-release formulation helps most people tolerate it well.

✅ Long-term protection Metformin has decades of safety data and is protective against type 2 diabetes. For someone with severe insulin resistance and PCOS, it is one of the most important metabolic tools available.

💉 GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (e.g., Ozempic®/Semaglutide)

GLP-1 agonists are a newer class of medication that mimic a natural gut hormone called GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1). Semaglutide (Ozempic®, Wegovy® in Canada) is the most well-known. Though approved primarily for type 2 diabetes and weight management, there is growing evidence supporting their use in PCOS — particularly for those with significant insulin resistance.

Research shows that GLP-1 agonists can lower androgen levels, restore regular periods, and improve fertility in women with PCOS and obesity. The weight loss achieved with these medications can be transformative for the entire hormonal and metabolic picture.

🥗 Dietary Changes — Your Most Powerful Daily Tool

Food is medicine in PCOS — particularly when insulin resistance and inflammation are the core problems. No single "PCOS diet" works for everyone, but the following principles are backed by strong evidence:

Reduce the Insulin Load of Your Meals

  • Choose lower glycaemic index (GI) carbohydrates — whole grains, legumes, and non-starchy vegetables cause a slower, smaller rise in blood sugar compared to white bread, white rice, sugary drinks, and processed snacks
  • Pair carbohydrates with protein, healthy fat, or fibre — this further slows glucose absorption and flattens the post-meal insulin spike
  • Reduce ultra-processed foods and added sugars — these drive insulin spikes and fuel inflammation
  • Consider time-restricted eating (eating within a consistent 8–10 hour window) — early evidence suggests this can improve insulin sensitivity in PCOS

Reduce Inflammation Through Food

  • Increase omega-3 rich foods: fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts
  • Eat a wide variety of colourful vegetables and berries — the pigments (polyphenols) are powerful anti-inflammatory agents
  • Use olive oil as your primary cooking fat
  • Reduce refined vegetable oils, fried foods, and processed meats
  • Limit alcohol — it raises triglycerides, worsens liver fat, and promotes inflammation
✅ Even modest changes make a real difference Research shows that even a 5–10% reduction in body weight in women with PCOS can significantly lower androgen levels, restore regular periods, and improve insulin sensitivity — independent of how that weight is lost. You do not need to reach an "ideal" weight to see meaningful improvements.

🏃 Exercise — A Direct Insulin-Sensitising Drug

Exercise works like a medication for insulin resistance. When your muscles contract, they can absorb glucose from the bloodstream without needing insulin — directly improving the problem at the cellular level. This effect lasts for hours after each session and accumulates with consistent training.

What Happens When Insulin Resistance Improves?

This is the most exciting part of PCOS management. Because insulin resistance sits at the centre of the hormonal and inflammatory cycle, improving it sets off a cascade of positive changes throughout your body. Here is what you might notice as your insulin sensitivity improves:

✅ The positive cascade
  • Lower insulin levels → Lower androgen production — the ovaries receive less stimulation to make testosterone
  • Lower androgens → Fewer androgenic symptoms — gradual improvement in acne, excess hair, and scalp hair loss
  • Lower insulin → More regular ovulation — periods become more predictable; fertility improves
  • Reduced abdominal fat → Lower CRP — inflammation calms down throughout the body
  • Lower inflammation → Better insulin signalling — a virtuous cycle begins to replace the vicious one
  • Better energy, mood, and mental clarity — as blood sugar fluctuations stabilise
  • Reduced long-term risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and endometrial (uterine lining) cancer
