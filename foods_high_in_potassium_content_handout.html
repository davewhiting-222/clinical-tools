Foods High in Potassium: What to Watch Out For

Prepared by your family doctor July 29, 2026

Why Does Potassium Matter?

Potassium is a mineral (a naturally occurring nutrient) your body needs to keep your muscles and heart working properly. For most people, eating potassium-rich foods is a healthy habit. However, if your kidneys are not working at full strength, or if you are taking certain medications, potassium can build up to dangerous levels in your blood.

⚠️ High Potassium (Hyperkalemia) Can Be Serious High potassium in the blood — called hyperkalemia (high-per-kuh-LEE-mee-ah) — can cause dangerous heart rhythm problems, muscle weakness, and in severe cases can be life-threatening. Your doctor has given you this handout because managing your potassium intake matters for your health.
📋 Who Needs to Limit Potassium? You may need to limit potassium if you have: chronic kidney disease (CKD), are on dialysis, take medications such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or potassium-sparing diuretics (water pills), or your blood test has shown that your potassium level is already elevated. Always follow the specific target your doctor or dietitian gives you.

How Much Potassium Is Allowed?

A general healthy adult diet contains about 3,500–4,700 mg of potassium per day. If you have been asked to restrict potassium, your target is usually 1,500–2,000 mg per day, though your doctor or registered dietitian will give you your personal limit.

🔔 Ask Before You Use Salt Substitutes Many salt substitutes (such as No Salt® or Nu-Salt®) are made almost entirely of potassium chloride. Using these can raise your blood potassium to dangerous levels very quickly. Do not use salt substitutes without checking with your doctor first.

The Leaching Trick: Reducing Potassium in Vegetables

Leaching (LEE-ching) is a cooking technique that draws potassium out of vegetables before you eat them. It will not remove all the potassium, but it can significantly reduce the amount — especially helpful for potatoes and root vegetables.

✅ How to Leach Vegetables (e.g., Potatoes)
  1. Peel the vegetable and cut it into small pieces (no thicker than 1 cm / ½ inch).
  2. Rinse with cold water for 30 seconds.
  3. Soak in a large pot of warm water (10 parts water to 1 part vegetable) for at least 2 hours, or overnight in the fridge.
  4. Drain and rinse again with warm water.
  5. Cook in a fresh pot of unsalted water — do not cook in the soaking water.

This method can reduce potassium content by up to 50% in potatoes.3

🔔 Cooking Method Matters
  • Boiling in a large volume of water reduces potassium more than steaming or microwaving.
  • Canned vegetables — drain and rinse the liquid. The liquid is very high in potassium.
  • Avoid soups, stews, and juices made with high-potassium vegetables — the potassium dissolves into the liquid, which you then drink.

Hidden Sources of Potassium to Watch For

Potassium can hide in surprising places. Check labels on packaged foods and ask about ingredients when eating out.

  • 🧂 Salt substitutes — almost pure potassium chloride (extremely dangerous)
  • 🥤 Sports drinks (e.g., Gatorade®, Powerade®) — contain added potassium electrolytes
  • 🥛 Meal replacement drinks (e.g., Ensure®, Boost®) — often fortified with potassium
  • 🌿 Herbal teas and supplements — some contain high amounts (e.g., dandelion, nettle)
  • 🍅 Tomato-based sauces, soups, and ketchup — concentrated potassium
  • 🫘 Baked beans and bean-based dips (hummus, refried beans)
  • 🥜 Peanut butter and nut butters — 2 tbsp contains ~180–200 mg
  • 🍫 Chocolate and cocoa powder — significant potassium per serving
  • 💊 Some medications and supplements — always tell your pharmacist you are on a potassium restriction

Your Action Plan: Daily Checklist

Use this checklist each day to help keep your potassium in a safe range.

  • Know my personal daily potassium limit (ask my doctor or dietitian to write it here: _______ mg/day)
  • Avoid or strictly limit 🔴 Very High potassium foods listed in the table above
  • Choose 🟢 Lower potassium alternatives whenever possible
  • Leach potatoes and root vegetables before cooking
  • Boil vegetables in large amounts of water and discard cooking liquid
  • Drain and rinse canned vegetables and legumes (beans) before eating
  • Check food labels for potassium chloride, particularly in low-sodium products
  • Avoid salt substitutes completely unless specifically approved by my doctor
  • Tell my pharmacist I need to limit potassium before starting any new medication or supplement
  • Keep my next blood test appointment to monitor my potassium level
  • Ask for a referral to a registered dietitian (RD) for a personalized meal plan

A Note of Encouragement

💜 You Can Still Eat Well

A low-potassium diet can feel overwhelming at first, especially when many "healthy" foods are on the limit list. But this does not mean your diet has to be bland or boring. With the right swaps and cooking techniques, you can still enjoy a wide variety of satisfying, nutritious foods.
