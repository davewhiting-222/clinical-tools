Understanding Atrial Fibrillation: Your Guide to Management

Prepared by your family doctor
June 9, 2026
What You Need to Know: Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common heart rhythm disorder that affects over 350,000 Canadians. While it can feel concerning, with proper understanding and management, most people with AF live healthy, active lives.

What is Atrial Fibrillation?

Atrial fibrillation is an irregular heart rhythm where the upper chambers of your heart (the atria) beat chaotically instead of in a coordinated way. Instead of a steady "lub-dub," your heart may feel like it's racing, fluttering, or skipping beats.

Normally, your heart beats 60-100 times per minute in a regular pattern. With AF, your heart rate can be anywhere from 60 to over 175 beats per minute, and the rhythm is unpredictable.

What Causes Atrial Fibrillation?

AF happens when the electrical signals in your heart become disorganized. Think of it like static interference on a radio - the normal signal gets scrambled. This can occur due to:

  • Age-related changes: Heart tissue naturally changes as we age
  • Heart conditions: High blood pressure, heart disease, or heart valve problems
  • Other medical conditions: Thyroid disorders, diabetes, or sleep apnea
  • Lifestyle factors: Excessive alcohol, stimulants, or high stress
  • Sometimes: No clear cause can be identified (called "lone AF")

Risk Factors for Developing AF

Risk Factors You Cannot Change

  • Age: Risk increases significantly after age 65
  • Family history: Genetics play a role in some cases
  • Gender: Men develop AF slightly more often than women
  • Race: More common in people of European descent

Risk Factors You Can Influence

  • High blood pressure (most important modifiable risk factor)
  • Obesity
  • Diabetes
  • Sleep apnea
  • Alcohol consumption
  • Smoking
  • Physical inactivity
  • Stress levels

Lifestyle Changes to Reduce Your AF Risk

Alcohol: The Most Important Lifestyle Factor

Why it matters: Alcohol is one of the strongest modifiable triggers for AF. Even moderate drinking can increase your risk, and binge drinking dramatically raises your chances of an AF episode.

Action steps:

  • Limit alcohol to no more than 1-2 standard drinks per week
  • Avoid binge drinking (more than 4-5 drinks in one session)
  • Consider alcohol-free days or weeks to see if your symptoms improve
  • Track your AF episodes in relation to alcohol consumption

Other Key Lifestyle Modifications

Area Specific Actions Why It Helps
Weight Management Aim for BMI under 27; lose 10% of body weight if overweight Reduces strain on heart and improves electrical stability
Blood Pressure Target under 130/80; take medications as prescribed High BP is the #1 risk factor for developing AF

Stress Management Regular relaxation, meditation, or counselling Chronic stress can trigger irregular heart rhythms

How We Treat Atrial Fibrillation

AF treatment focuses on three main goals: controlling symptoms, preventing complications (especially stroke), and improving quality of life.

Treatment Approaches

1. Rate Control

We slow down your heart rate to a more comfortable level (usually 60-100 beats per minute at rest) using medications like:

  • Beta-blockers (metoprolol, bisoprolol)
  • Calcium channel blockers (diltiazem, verapamil)
  • Digoxin (in specific cases)

2. Rhythm Control

We try to restore and maintain a normal heart rhythm using:

  • Antiarrhythmic medications (amiodarone, flecainide, propafenone)
  • Electrical cardioversion (controlled shock to reset rhythm)
  • Catheter ablation (procedure to destroy problematic heart tissue)

Anticoagulation: Preventing Stroke

Critical Information: People with AF have a 5-fold increased risk of stroke. This is because blood can pool in the irregular-beating atria and form clots that can travel to the brain.

How We Determine Your Stroke Risk

We use the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score to calculate your personal stroke risk:

Risk Factor Points
Congestive heart failure 1
Hypertension 1
Age 75 or older 2
Diabetes 1
Stroke/TIA history 2
Vascular disease 1
Age 65-74 1
Sex category (female) 1

Anticoagulation Recommendations

  • Score 0 (men) or 1 (women): Usually no anticoagulation needed
  • Score 1 (men): Consider anticoagulation based on individual factors
  • Score 2 or higher: Anticoagulation strongly recommended

Types of Anticoagulants

Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) - Preferred Option

Available in Canada:

  • Apixaban (Eliquis): Taken twice daily
  • Rivaroxaban (Xarelto): Taken once daily
  • Dabigatran (Pradaxa): Taken twice daily
  • Edoxaban (Lixiana): Taken once daily

Advantages: No regular blood tests needed, fewer drug interactions, lower risk of brain bleeding

Warfarin (Coumadin) - Traditional Option

When used: When DOACs aren't suitable or with certain valve conditions

Requires: Regular blood tests (INR) to monitor dosing, target INR 2.0-3.0

Considerations: Many food and drug interactions

Living with Anticoagulants: Safety Tips

Daily Safety Checklist

  • Take your anticoagulant at the same time each day
  • Use a soft-bristled toothbrush and electric razor
  • Wear gloves when gardening or doing rough work
  • Avoid contact sports or activities with high fall risk
  • Tell all healthcare providers you take anticoagulants
  • Seek immediate care for unusual bleeding or severe headaches

When to Contact Your Healthcare Team

Call 911 immediately if you experience:
  • Severe chest pain
  • Sudden severe headache
  • Signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty)
  • Major bleeding that won't stop
  • Heart rate over 150 beats per minute with symptoms
Contact your doctor within 24 hours for:
  • New or worsening shortness of breath
  • Dizziness or fainting spells
  • Unusual fatigue or weakness
  • Prolonged palpitations
  • Unusual bruising or bleeding on anticoagulants

Your AF Management Action Plan

Weekly Checklist

  1. Check your pulse daily and record any irregularities
  2. Take all medications as prescribed
  3. Monitor alcohol intake (aim for ≤2 drinks per week)
  4. Maintain regular sleep schedule (7-9 hours)
  5. Engage in moderate exercise as recommended
  6. Practice stress management techniques
  7. Monitor weight if overweight
Remember: Atrial fibrillation is a manageable condition. Many people with AF live full, active lives. The key is working closely with your healthcare team, taking medications as prescribed, and making healthy lifestyle choices. You're not alone in this journey.
