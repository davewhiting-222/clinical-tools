Understanding Fatigue in Adults 65–75

Why You Feel Tired — And What Can Actually Be Done About It

Why This Matters

Persistent fatigue is one of the most common complaints in the 65–75 age group — but it is often dismissed as a simple consequence of aging. In reality, fatigue at this life stage usually has several identifiable, treatable causes. Standard blood work alone will not always find them. This handout explains the five most common underlying drivers, as well as the important medical conditions your doctor will want to rule out.

The Five Key Drivers of Fatigue in This Age Group

Research has identified a set of factors that are particularly common — and often overlooked — in adults between 65 and 75. These causes may not show up on routine testing, but they are real, they are meaningful, and most of them are addressable.1,2

1

Metabolic Inefficiency — "Energy Friction" at the Cellular Level

Even without a diagnosis of diabetes, many people in this age group develop a gradual decline in the efficiency with which the body converts food into energy. This happens for two related reasons:3,4

  • Mitochondrial decline: Mitochondria are the microscopic "power plants" inside your muscle cells. With age, they become fewer and less efficient, meaning the body has to work harder to generate the same amount of daily energy — like driving a car with a partially blocked fuel line.
  • Subclinical insulin resistance: Muscle cells gradually become less responsive to insulin, making it harder to move glucose (your body's fuel) into cells where it can be used. Blood sugar may still be "normal" on a standard test, but the underlying metabolic friction is already causing fatigue.5

What Helps

Regular physical activity (especially resistance training), reducing refined carbohydrates, and maintaining a healthy weight all meaningfully improve mitochondrial function and insulin sensitivity — often with noticeable improvements in energy within weeks.3,5

2

Chronic Low-Grade Inflammation ("Inflammaging")

Aging is associated with a state of persistent, low-level systemic inflammation — a phenomenon researchers call "inflammaging." This is not an acute infection or illness, but a constant background hum of pro-inflammatory signals circulating in the body.6,7

This chronic inflammation can directly cause what scientists call "sickness behaviour" — the same lethargy, physical heaviness, reduced motivation, and desire to withdraw that you feel when fighting a flu. When this state is chronic rather than temporary, it manifests as persistent, unexplained exhaustion.6

What Helps

Anti-inflammatory lifestyle changes — regular exercise, a Mediterranean-style diet rich in vegetables and healthy fats, adequate sleep, and stress management — have been shown to meaningfully reduce inflammatory markers and improve energy levels.6,7

3

Existential Burnout and Loss of Purpose

The transition into the 65–75 age bracket is one of the most psychologically complex periods of adult life. Retirement, shifts in family roles, and the natural process of "life review" — looking back and evaluating whether life has gone as hoped — can create a form of motivational fatigue that is just as real and physically felt as any medical cause of tiredness.8

A loss of structured identity (career, active parenting) or a sense of disconnection from purpose can manifest as persistent low energy, difficulty getting out of bed, and a feeling of physical heaviness. This is not a character flaw — it is a recognized psychological and biological phenomenon. Loneliness and social isolation compound this significantly.8

What Helps

Identifying meaningful activities, maintaining social connections, and talking with a counsellor or psychologist can be profoundly effective. This form of fatigue responds to engagement, purpose, and connection — not rest.8

4

Sleep Fragmentation — Poor Quality Despite Adequate Hours

Many people in this age group feel they are getting enough sleep — perhaps 7 to 8 hours — yet still wake exhausted. The problem is often not the quantity of sleep but its quality. Several age-related changes disrupt the deep, restorative stages of sleep:9

  • Nocturia: Waking once or more to use the bathroom is very common and prevents consolidated, restorative sleep cycles.
  • Chronic pain: Even mild discomfort — a sore hip, back pain — can cause micro-arousals that fragment sleep architecture without fully waking you.
  • Circadian drift: The body's internal clock shifts earlier with age, leading to early-morning waking and a reduction in deep (slow-wave) sleep and REM sleep.
  • Undiagnosed sleep apnea: Particularly common in this age group and often unrecognized — ask your doctor if you snore or wake unrefreshed.

What Helps

Addressing the underlying cause (nocturia, pain, sleep apnea) is key. Consistent sleep and wake times, limiting fluids in the evening, and good sleep hygiene significantly improve sleep quality. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is more effective than sleep medications for long-term improvement.9

5

Medication Sensitivity — Iatrogenic (Medication-Caused) Fatigue

By the time most people reach 65–75, they are often managing several chronic conditions and taking multiple medications. What many people don't realize is that age-related changes in body composition — less muscle, more fat, slower kidney and liver function — mean that medications are processed more slowly and stay in the system longer than they did at younger ages.10,11

Common culprits include:10,11

  • Blood pressure medications (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers) — can cause fatigue and exercise intolerance
  • Statins (cholesterol-lowering medications) — a recognized, though often underreported, cause of muscle fatigue and weakness
  • Over-the-counter antihistamines (e.g., diphenhydramine, found in many sleep aids and allergy medications) — particularly sedating in older adults and can cause prolonged daytime grogginess
  • Benzodiazepines and sleep medications — hang-over effects are more pronounced and longer-lasting
  • Antidepressants — some cause fatigue as a side effect

What Helps

A careful medication review with your doctor — sometimes called a "brown bag review" — can identify medications contributing to fatigue. Never stop or change medications without consulting your doctor, but this review is absolutely worth requesting.10,11

The Five Drivers of Fatigue in the 65–75 Age Group

In most people, two or more of these drivers are present simultaneously. Addressing them in combination produces greater improvements than treating any single factor in isolation.

Ruling Out Medical Causes: The "Usual Suspects"

Alongside the five drivers above, your doctor will want to ensure that certain standard medical conditions are not contributing to your fatigue. These should be investigated with blood tests and clinical assessment. Several of these are straightforward to identify and treat.12

🩸
Anaemia (Iron deficiency)
Low iron reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen to tissues, causing profound fatigue.
🧪
Vitamin B12 & Vitamin D Deficiency
Both are common in this age group and both cause fatigue, weakness, and cognitive fog.
🦋
Thyroid Dysfunction
An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) is a classic, easily missed cause of fatigue — detected with a simple blood test (TSH).
❤️
Cardiovascular & Lung Conditions
Early heart failure, atrial fibrillation (irregular heartbeat), or COPD reduce the body's ability to deliver oxygen during activity.
🔬
Hidden Infections
Urinary tract infections and post-viral fatigue can be surprisingly subtle in older adults — sometimes presenting only as tiredness or confusion.
⚠️
Occult Malignancy
Unexplained weight loss, drenching night sweats, or new lymph node swelling alongside fatigue always warrants investigation.

When to See Your Doctor Promptly

Contact your healthcare provider sooner rather than later if your fatigue is accompanied by any of the following:

  • Unintentional weight loss of more than 3–4 kg over several months
  • Drenching night sweats
  • Significant shortness of breath with minimal exertion
  • Chest pain or palpitations
  • Swollen lymph nodes
  • Confusion or memory changes
  • Fatigue that has appeared suddenly and is severe

A Practical Path Forward

Because fatigue in this age group is usually multi-factorial, the most effective approach is systematic. The conversation with your doctor is best structured around three questions: Have we ruled out the serious medical causes? Which of the five lifestyle drivers are most relevant for me? What are the two or three most impactful changes I can make?

What the Evidence Supports

The single most reliably effective intervention for fatigue in adults 65–75 — with evidence across almost all of the drivers above — is regular physical activity. Exercise improves mitochondrial function, reduces inflammaging, improves sleep quality, boosts mood and sense of purpose, and even enhances medication metabolism. Even modest amounts — a 30-minute brisk walk most days — produce meaningful improvements in energy, often within 4–6 weeks.3,6,7

Diet quality, sleep hygiene, social engagement, and medication review are the other high-yield areas. None of these require a prescription — but all of them benefit from a conversation with your healthcare provider.

The Bottom Line

Persistent fatigue in the 65–75 age group is common — but it is not simply "getting older." In most cases, there are identifiable, modifiable contributing factors. A thorough assessment combining standard blood tests with an honest conversation about sleep, medications, diet, activity, and mental wellbeing can reveal the picture. The good news is that most of these causes respond to targeted changes, and many people in this age group experience significant, meaningful improvements in energy and quality of life.
