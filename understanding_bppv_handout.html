Understanding BPPV

Prepared by your family doctor August 20, 2026

What Is BPPV?

BPPV stands for Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo. It sounds complicated, but let's break it down:

  • Benign — not dangerous or life-threatening
  • Paroxysmal — comes on suddenly, in short bursts
  • Positional — triggered by head position or movement
  • Vertigo — a spinning sensation, as if the room is moving around you

BPPV is the most common cause of vertigo (dizziness with a spinning feeling) and accounts for nearly half of all dizziness cases seen by doctors.1 The good news is that it is very treatable — often in just one or two visits.

💡 Key Point BPPV is extremely common. It affects about 2.4% of people at some point in their lives and becomes more frequent as we age.2 You are not alone, and this is very manageable.

How Your Inner Ear Normally Works

To understand BPPV, it helps to know a little about your inner ear. Deep inside each ear, there are tiny fluid-filled canals — called semicircular canals — that sense movement and help your brain know which way is up.

Also inside the ear, there are microscopic crystals made of calcium carbonate called otoliths (or "otoconia"), which literally means "ear stones." These crystals sit in a specific area and normally help you sense gravity and straight-line movement (like going up in an elevator).

What Causes BPPV?

BPPV happens when one or more of those calcium crystals (otoconia) break loose and drift into one of the semicircular canals — where they don't belong. When your head moves, the loose crystals shift around and send confusing signals to your brain, making you feel like you're spinning even when you're not.3

Common Triggers and Risk Factors

Often there is no single clear cause, but some things can increase the chances of crystals breaking loose:

Risk Factor Why It Matters
Older age Crystals naturally become less stable as we age; BPPV is most common in those over 502
Head injury or trauma A bump to the head can dislodge crystals
Prolonged bed rest or staying still Being inactive (e.g., after surgery or illness) is a known trigger
Inner ear infection (vestibular neuritis) Inflammation can loosen crystals
Low vitamin D Some evidence links vitamin D deficiency to more frequent BPPV4
Female sex Women are affected about twice as often as men2
Osteoporosis May affect crystal stability in the inner ear4
🧠 Worth Knowing In many cases — especially a first episode — no specific cause is ever found. This is completely normal. BPPV can just happen. It does not mean something is seriously wrong with your brain or ears.

What Does BPPV Feel Like?

The hallmark of BPPV is a sudden, intense spinning sensation that:

  • Comes on with specific head movements (rolling over in bed, looking up, bending down, tilting the head)
  • Starts within seconds of moving
  • Typically lasts less than one minute (usually 10–30 seconds)
  • May be accompanied by nausea, and occasionally vomiting
  • Settles quickly when you stop moving
  • Can return when you repeat the same movement

You may also feel:

  • Unsteady or "off balance" between spinning episodes
  • Lightheaded or woozy for a short time after an episode
  • Anxious or fatigued, especially if episodes are frequent
🚨 When to Seek Urgent Care The following symptoms are not typical of BPPV and need prompt medical attention. Please go to the emergency department or call 911 if you have:
  • Vertigo that comes on suddenly with a severe headache or the "worst headache of your life"
  • Double vision, slurred speech, trouble swallowing
  • Weakness or numbness in your face, arm, or leg
  • Vertigo lasting hours without stopping
  • Significant hearing loss or ringing in both ears at once
  • Difficulty walking or loss of coordination

How Is BPPV Diagnosed?

Your doctor can usually diagnose BPPV through a careful history and a simple bedside test — no blood tests, MRI, or special equipment are needed in most cases.

The Dix-Hallpike Test

The most common diagnostic test is called the Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre. Your doctor will guide you from a sitting to a lying-down position while turning your head to one side. If BPPV is present, this position briefly triggers your dizziness and causes a telltale eye movement called nystagmus (rapid, involuntary eye movements), which helps confirm the diagnosis.3

The test is done quickly, and while it may briefly reproduce your symptoms, it is safe and gives your doctor important information about which ear and which canal is affected.

💡 Good to Know There are different types of BPPV depending on which canal the crystals have moved into. The most common type involves the posterior semicircular canal, making up about 85–90% of cases.3 Your doctor will determine the type before recommending treatment.

How Is BPPV Treated?

The main treatment for BPPV is a series of guided head movements performed by your doctor or physiotherapist. These movements are designed to gently guide the displaced crystals back to where they belong. This is called a canalith repositioning manoeuvre.

The Epley Manoeuvre (Most Common Treatment)

For the most common type of BPPV (posterior canal), the Epley manoeuvre is the first-line treatment.5 It involves a sequence of four slow head-position changes, each held for about 30 seconds. Research shows it is effective in about 80–90% of cases after one or two sessions.5

✅ Treatment Success The Epley manoeuvre has a very high success rate. Many people feel significantly better — or completely symptom-free — after a single treatment session. A second session, if needed, brings the overall success rate even higher.

Other Manoeuvres

Depending on which canal is involved, your provider may use a different technique:

  • Semont manoeuvre — an alternative for posterior canal BPPV
  • Barbecue roll (Log Roll) — used for horizontal canal BPPV
  • Deep head-hanging manoeuvre — occasionally used for anterior canal BPPV

What About Medication?

Medications do not fix the underlying problem (the displaced crystals), but they may be used short-term to manage nausea during episodes. Common options include dimenhydrinate (Gravol®) for nausea. Your doctor may recommend this for comfort while arranging treatment, but repositioning manoeuvres remain the main treatment.6

⚠️ Avoid Long-Term Medication Use Medications like Gravol® can cause sedation and are not a long-term solution for BPPV. They suppress your balance system, which can actually slow recovery. The repositioning manoeuvre — not medication — is the evidence-based treatment of choice.6

Home Exercises: The Brandt-Daroff Exercises

After a repositioning manoeuvre, or in milder cases, your doctor or physiotherapist may teach you Brandt-Daroff exercises to do at home. These involve moving in and out of positions that briefly bring on your dizziness — helping your brain adapt over time. Ask your provider before starting these on your own, as the correct technique depends on which canal is affected.6

Your BPPV Action Plan

Here is a simple checklist to guide you through managing your BPPV:

  • Tell your doctor about all your symptoms, including exactly which head movements bring on dizziness
  • Undergo the Dix-Hallpike (or roll test) in office so your doctor can confirm the diagnosis and affected side
  • Have the Epley manoeuvre (or appropriate repositioning manoeuvre) performed by your doctor or physiotherapist
  • Move slowly and carefully when getting in and out of bed, especially in the first few days after treatment
  • Ask if a referral to a physiotherapist experienced in vestibular rehabilitation is right for you
  • Discuss whether your vitamin D levels should be checked (low vitamin D may increase recurrence risk)4
  • Know the warning signs (listed above) that would warrant urgent care
  • Follow up with your doctor if symptoms return — BPPV can recur in about 15–50% of people within 1–5 years7

Safety Tips at Home During Episodes

1
Sit down immediately when you feel a spinning episode coming on to prevent falls.
2
Grip a stable surface — a countertop, wall, or sturdy chair — until the spinning passes.
3
Move slowly and deliberately, especially when getting out of bed. Roll to your side first, then push yourself up.
4
Avoid driving if you are having frequent or unpredictable episodes of vertigo.
5
Keep your home safe: remove loose rugs, use night lights, and keep pathways clear to reduce fall risk.

Will BPPV Come Back?

BPPV can recur. Roughly 15–50% of people experience another episode within one to five years.7 This is not a sign that the treatment failed — it simply means that crystals have shifted again. The good news is that treatment works just as well the second (or third) time around.

If your BPPV recurs frequently, your doctor may look for underlying contributing factors, such as low vitamin D, osteoporosis, or inner ear conditions, and address those directly.

🧠 A Word of Reassurance BPPV, while disorienting and sometimes frightening, is not a sign of a stroke, brain tumour, or serious neurological disease. It is a mechanical problem — like a small pebble rolling to where it shouldn't be — and it responds very well to the right physical treatment. Most people return to full normal activity quickly.

Physiotherapy and Vestibular Rehabilitation

A physiotherapist with training in vestibular rehabilitation can be a valuable part of your care, especially if:

  • Your BPPV is recurrent or hard to treat
  • You continue to feel unsteady between episodes
  • You are at risk of falls
  • You need guided home exercise instruction

Vestibular physiotherapy is available in Kelowna and the surrounding Okanagan area. Ask your doctor for a referral if you feel this would benefit you. In BC, you can also self-refer to many private physiotherapy clinics.
