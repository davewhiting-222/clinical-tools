Understanding Your Atypical Papillary Breast Lesion

Prepared by your family doctor
What This Means: Your biopsy shows abnormal breast tissue that needs further evaluation to determine whether it's benign (non-cancerous) or early cancer. This finding requires additional steps, but many atypical papillary lesions turn out to be non-cancerous.

Understanding Your Results

The tissue taken from the lump in the upper-outer part of your right breast shows an abnormal growth that looks like a papillary (finger-like) pattern. It is called an "atypical papillary lesion." This means the cells are not normal, but the test cannot yet tell whether they are a non-cancerous growth or an early form of breast cancer.

The cells are hormone-sensitive (they have estrogen receptors) and do not show features that are typical of the more aggressive breast cancers.

What "Atypical" Means

  • Not normal: The cells look different from healthy breast tissue
  • Not clearly cancer: The sample is too small to make a definitive diagnosis
  • Needs more testing: A larger tissue sample is required for accurate diagnosis
  • Hormone-responsive: The cells have estrogen receptors, which is actually a positive finding

What Happens Next

We will arrange a short-term breast imaging study (ultrasound or mammogram) to see how the area looks in the whole breast. Most doctors recommend that the entire lesion be removed surgically – a small operation called an excisional biopsy – so the whole abnormal area can be examined.

Timeline Overview:
  • Imaging study: Within 1-2 weeks
  • Surgical consultation: Within 2-3 weeks
  • Excisional biopsy: Within 4-6 weeks
  • Final pathology results: 1-2 weeks after surgery

The Surgical Process

After the surgery, the pathologist will look at the whole piece of tissue and tell us whether it is truly cancer or a benign lesion. We will then meet with the breast-cancer team to discuss the results and any further treatment, such as hormone therapy, if needed. This process usually takes a few weeks from the imaging appointment to the final pathology report.

How You Might Be Feeling

Finding out there is an abnormal growth can feel scary, confusing, or upsetting. It's completely normal to have a mix of emotions right now, and you do not have to go through this alone.

Remember: Many atypical papillary lesions are benign. Taking these next steps is about getting accurate information, not because we assume the worst.

Common Emotions and Reactions

  • Anxiety: Worrying about what the results might show
  • Uncertainty: Not knowing what to expect next
  • Impatience: Wanting answers immediately
  • Fear: Concerns about cancer or treatment

These feelings are normal and valid. Consider reaching out to family, friends, or a counselor for emotional support during this time.

Questions to Ask Your Care Team

Come prepared to your appointments. Write down your questions beforehand and bring a support person if helpful.

What You Can Do Now

Focus on what you can control:
  • Attend all scheduled appointments
  • Ask questions when you have them
  • Maintain your regular healthy habits
  • Reach out for support when needed
  • Avoid excessive internet searching about worst-case scenarios

Preparing for Your Next Appointment

  1. Write down any symptoms or changes you notice
  2. List all medications and supplements you take
  3. Prepare your questions in advance
  4. Bring a support person if it would help
  5. Bring your health card and any previous imaging results
When to Contact Your Healthcare Team Immediately:
  • New or worsening breast pain
  • Changes in the breast lump or surrounding area
  • New lumps in the breast or armpit
  • Nipple discharge or changes
  • Skin changes on the breast
