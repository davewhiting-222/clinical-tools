Molluscum Contagiosum in Children

What Parents Need to Know: A Common Condition That Usually Resolves on Its Own

The Most Important Message

Molluscum contagiosum is a benign, self-limiting viral skin condition that is extremely common in childhood. In healthy children, the preferred approach is watchful waiting — the infection will clear on its own, usually within 6 to 18 months, without any treatment at all. This is the approach recommended by most dermatology and pediatric guidelines worldwide.

What Is Molluscum Contagiosum?

Molluscum contagiosum is a common viral skin infection caused by the Molluscum contagiosum virus (MCV), a member of the poxvirus family. It produces small, round bumps on the skin that are typically flesh-coloured, pearly, or slightly pink, with a characteristic dimple or pit at the centre (called an umbilication).1,2

The bumps — called "mollusca" or "papules" — are usually 2 to 5 millimetres in diameter (roughly the size of a pencil tip to a small pea). They are generally painless, though they can sometimes become itchy, red, or inflamed, particularly as the immune system begins fighting the virus.1,2

Each papule has a characteristic central dimple (umbilication) and contains a core of viral material. In children, lesions most commonly appear on the trunk, arms, armpits, and neck.

In children, molluscum papules most commonly appear on the trunk, face, arms, and in the folds of the skin such as the armpits and behind the knees. Unlike adults, where the condition is often sexually transmitted and appears in the genital region, childhood molluscum is typically spread through skin-to-skin contact or sharing towels and bath items — not through sexual activity.2,3

How Common Is It?

Molluscum contagiosum is one of the most common viral skin infections in children worldwide. Studies estimate that it affects up to 5–8% of children at any given time, though the true prevalence is likely higher because many cases go undiagnosed or resolve before a child sees a doctor.1,4 It is seen most frequently in children between the ages of 1 and 10, with peak incidence in the preschool and early school years.1,2

How Is It Spread? (Infectivity)

The molluscum contagiosum virus spreads through direct contact with the virus. There are several ways this can happen:2,3,5

  • Direct skin-to-skin contact: Touching or playing with another child who has molluscum is the most common route of transmission.
  • Fomites (shared objects): The virus can survive on surfaces and objects such as towels, flannels, clothing, bath sponges, and sports equipment.
  • Autoinoculation: Children can spread the virus to other parts of their own body by scratching or touching their lesions and then touching unaffected skin.
  • Water: Evidence suggests transmission can occur in swimming pools and shared baths, likely through contact with contaminated water or pool equipment.5

How Contagious Is It, Really?

Molluscum is contagious, but it is not highly contagious in the way that chickenpox or influenza are. The virus requires fairly direct or sustained contact to spread. Children with molluscum do not need to be excluded from school, daycare, or sports, though covering visible lesions with clothing or a waterproof bandage during contact sports is a reasonable precaution. The risk of transmission in a typical classroom setting is low.3,5

Reducing the Spread at Home

The following measures can help reduce spread within a household and to other areas of your child's body:3,5

  • Avoid sharing towels, washcloths, or bath sponges
  • Encourage your child not to scratch or pick at the bumps
  • Cover lesions with clothing or a waterproof dressing during swimming or contact activities
  • Wash hands regularly, particularly after touching affected areas
  • Avoid shaving over affected areas (in older children)

What Is the Natural History? (What Happens Without Treatment)

This is the most important thing to understand about molluscum contagiosum: in children with a healthy immune system, the condition will resolve completely on its own without any treatment.6,7

Most children experience complete resolution within 12–18 months, though some take up to 24 months or longer. Importantly, the immune system clears the infection without leaving permanent marks in most cases.

What to Expect During Natural Resolution

Understanding the natural course helps parents know what to expect:6,7,8

  • Incubation period: After exposure, it takes 2 to 8 weeks (and sometimes up to 6 months) before any bumps appear.
  • Spreading phase: New lesions may continue to appear for weeks to months as the virus spreads through autoinoculation. This is normal and does not mean things are getting worse.
  • Number of lesions: Most children develop between 1 and 20 bumps, though some have more. Clusters are common.
  • Inflammation as a good sign: Often, just before the bumps resolve, they become red, swollen, or crusted. This inflammation represents the immune system mounting a response — it is actually a sign that resolution is near, not a sign of infection or complication.
  • Full resolution: The bumps disappear completely, generally without leaving any scars or marks, particularly if they are not scratched or picked.

What Research Tells Us About Waiting

A landmark randomized controlled trial (the SWET trial) published in the British Medical Journal found no significant benefit to active treatment over watchful waiting in children with molluscum. Rates of clearance were similar regardless of whether children were treated or left alone. This has informed guidelines from major dermatology and pediatric societies recommending watchful waiting as the preferred strategy in otherwise healthy children.8,9

Why Waiting Is the Preferred Approach

Watchful waiting (also called "active non-intervention") is recommended as the first-line approach for molluscum contagiosum in healthy children for several important reasons:7,8,9

  • The condition is self-limiting: Virtually all cases in healthy children resolve without treatment. Treating a condition that will resolve on its own exposes children to unnecessary discomfort or risk.
  • Treatments can be painful: Many of the available treatments (such as cryotherapy or curettage) are uncomfortable procedures. Putting a young child through a painful procedure for a condition that will clear up on its own is difficult to justify.
  • Treatments are not clearly superior to waiting: Clinical trials have not convincingly shown that any available treatment significantly shortens the course of molluscum compared to natural resolution in the majority of children.
  • Risk of scarring: Some treatments carry a risk of leaving small scars — an outcome that spontaneous resolution rarely produces.
  • Immune development: Allowing the immune system to clear the infection naturally may confer lasting immunity to future molluscum infection.

When to Return to Your Doctor

While watchful waiting is usually the right approach, please contact your healthcare provider if:1,2

  • Lesions become severely inflamed, crusted, or appear infected (yellow pus, significant pain)
  • Your child has eczema — molluscum can be more widespread and troublesome in children with eczema
  • Lesions appear near the eyes (risk of conjunctivitis)
  • The number of lesions is very large (hundreds of bumps can indicate an immune problem)
  • Your child's condition has not improved after 18–24 months
  • Your child or family is significantly distressed and wishes to discuss active treatment

Treatment Options (When Treatment Is Chosen)

If you and your healthcare provider decide that treatment is appropriate — for example, due to significant social or psychological distress, extensive spread, or involvement of a sensitive area — several options are available. It is important to know that none of these treatments have been proven clearly superior to watchful waiting, and all have potential drawbacks.8,9,10

Treatment How It Works Considerations
Cryotherapy (Freezing) Liquid nitrogen is applied to each lesion to freeze and destroy it. Effective for individual lesions. Painful — difficult to tolerate in young children. Requires clinic visit. Risk of blistering or hypopigmentation (light spots on skin).
Curettage (Scraping) Each lesion is scraped off using a small curette instrument, often after topical anaesthetic cream. Immediately removes lesions. Can be traumatic for young children even with anaesthetic. Risk of scarring. Requires a skilled hand.
Cantharadin (Beetle juice) A blistering agent applied to lesions in the clinic, causing them to blister and fall off. Painless when applied; blistering occurs 24–48 hrs later. Not licensed in Canada as a commercial product but is compounded and widely used. Generally well-tolerated in children. May cause large blisters.11
Topical Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) An alkaline solution applied at home to dissolve lesions. Can be applied by parents at home. Causes local irritation. Requires consistent daily application. Evidence supports modest effectiveness.10
Imiquimod Cream Stimulates the immune system to fight the virus when applied to lesions. Applied at home by parents. Randomized trials (including the SWET trial) have not shown it to be significantly better than placebo in children. Not currently recommended as first-line active treatment.8,9
Topical Salicylic Acid A keratolytic agent that gradually breaks down the lesion tissue. Available over-the-counter. Requires consistent application. Limited evidence for molluscum specifically. May cause skin irritation.
Benign Neglect (Watchful Waiting) No treatment — allow the immune system to clear the infection naturally. Recommended first-line approach. No pain, no procedures, no risk of scarring. Full resolution expected in 12–18 months in most children. Consistent with major clinical guidelines.7,8,9

A Note on Eczema and Molluscum

Children with eczema (atopic dermatitis) often have more widespread molluscum and may find it more challenging to resolve spontaneously. If your child has both conditions, a discussion with your doctor about the timing and appropriateness of treatment is worthwhile, as the risk-benefit calculation may differ from that of a child without eczema.12

Having the Conversation: What to Tell Your Child

It can be helpful to explain molluscum to your child in simple terms. You might say: "You have some tiny bumps on your skin caused by a virus — a bit like a very mild version of a cold, but on your skin. Your body knows how to fight it, and the bumps will go away on their own over time. You don't need any needles or painful treatments. In the meantime, try not to scratch them."

Children who understand that the bumps will go away without treatment are often less distressed by them — and parents who understand the natural history tend to feel more comfortable with a watchful waiting approach.

Summary: What You Need to Remember

Molluscum contagiosum is a common, harmless viral skin infection in children. It is contagious but not highly so, and children do not need to be kept home from school or excluded from activities. The condition resolves completely on its own in virtually all healthy children, usually within 6 to 18 months. Watchful waiting is the preferred and evidence-based approach because the available treatments have not been shown to significantly outperform the natural course of the condition, and they carry their own discomforts and risks. If treatment is desired for social or psychological reasons, several options exist and can be discussed with your doctor.

The Bottom Line for Parents

Your child's immune system is fully capable of clearing molluscum contagiosum. The bumps will go away. Doing nothing is not neglect — it is the evidence-based, guideline-recommended approach. Be patient, try to discourage scratching, and take comfort in knowing that this is one of those times when watchful waiting truly is the best medicine.
