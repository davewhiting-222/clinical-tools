Understanding Your Ear Biopsy Results: Basal Cell Carcinoma and Possible Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis

Prepared by your family doctor
April 14, 2026
What This Means: You have two different types of cells growing in the skin on the back of your right ear. One is a basal cell carcinoma (a common skin cancer), and the other may be a rare immune cell condition. We need more tests to understand exactly what we're dealing with and plan the best treatment for you.

What We Found in Your Biopsy

Basal Cell Carcinoma

This is the most common type of skin cancer. The good news is that basal cell carcinomas:

  • Grow slowly over months to years
  • Almost never spread to other parts of your body
  • Are highly curable when removed completely
  • Are more common in people who have had organ transplants due to immunosuppressive medications

Possible Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis (LCH)

This involves a buildup of special immune cells called Langerhans cells. In adults, LCH can:

  • Stay only in the skin (most common)
  • Sometimes affect other organs like lungs or bones
  • Range from very mild to needing treatment
  • Be harder to diagnose without special laboratory tests
Why We Need More Tests: The laboratory couldn't run a special test called CD1a that would help us confirm whether this is truly LCH or another similar condition. We need to send tissue to a specialized lab to get this answer.

Your Next Steps

Next steps

  • BC Cancer Agency consultation for surgical removal planning
  • BC Cancer Agency will decide regarding CD1a test on tissue sample (may be sent to US laboratory)
  • Blood tests to check overall health
  • Chest X-ray or CT scan to check if LCH affects other areas
  • Follow-up with your transplant team

Surgical Treatment

The surgeon will likely recommend removing the entire lesion with a procedure that:

  • Can usually be done as day surgery
  • Aims to preserve the shape and function of your ear
  • Provides more tissue for complete diagnosis
  • Ensures complete removal of the basal cell carcinoma

If LCH is Confirmed

Treatment options may include:

  • Watchful waiting: If only skin is involved and you feel well
  • Topical treatments: Special creams or light therapy
  • Systemic treatment: Medications if multiple organs are affected
  • Coordination with transplant team: Adjusting your immunosuppressive medications if needed

Important Questions to Ask Your Care Team

For Your Surgeon:

  • What are the benefits and risks of surgically removing the lesion right now?
  • How will you preserve the appearance and function of my ear?
  • What will the recovery process look like?

For Your Transplant Team:

  • How will these findings affect my liver transplant medications?
  • Do I need changes to my tacrolimus or other immunosuppressive drugs?
  • What additional monitoring will I need?

For All Specialists:

  • Will any additional imaging (CT scan, PET scan) be needed?
  • What signs should I watch for that would need urgent attention?
  • Are there support services you recommend for transplant patients dealing with skin cancers?

How You Might Be Feeling

It's completely normal to feel worried, confused, or upset hearing about two different growths, especially after having a transplant. Many people experience:

  • Anxiety about the unknown aspects of their diagnosis
  • Worry about how this affects their transplant health
  • Frustration about needing more tests and appointments
  • Fear about cancer, even though basal cell carcinoma has an excellent prognosis
Remember: You are not alone in this journey. Your medical team has experience with both skin cancers and rare conditions in transplant patients. We are here to support you every step of the way, and both conditions you're dealing with are very treatable.

Caring for Yourself

Skin Protection (Crucial for Transplant Patients)

  • Use broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 30+) daily, even in winter
  • Wear wide-brimmed hats and long sleeves when outdoors
  • Seek shade between 10 AM and 4 PM
  • Perform monthly self-skin exams
  • Report any new or changing spots to your dermatologist immediately

General Health

  • Take your transplant medications exactly as prescribed
  • Attend all scheduled follow-up appointments
  • Maintain good nutrition and regular exercise as tolerated
  • Stay up to date with vaccinations (as approved by your transplant team)
  • Consider counselling or support groups if feeling overwhelmed
When to Contact Your Doctor Immediately:
  • Any new lumps or bumps on your skin
  • Existing spots that change in size, color, or texture
  • Signs of infection at the biopsy site (increasing redness, warmth, discharge)
  • Unexplained fever, fatigue, or bone pain
  • Any concerns about your transplant medications or side effects
