Myth-Busters: Can I Do This Long Term?

Prepared by your family doctor
March 2026
The Bottom Line: Most nutritional strategies face sustainability challenges. The key isn't finding the "perfect" diet—it's finding one you can realistically maintain while living your life. Let's separate fact from fiction about long-term success.

The Reality Check: What the Research Shows

Here's what long-term studies actually tell us about diet sustainability:

Diet Type 2-Year Success Rate 5-Year Success Rate Main Challenge
Low-carb/Keto 40-50% 20-30% Social restrictions, food variety
Mediterranean 60-70% 45-55% Cost, meal preparation time
Intermittent Fasting 35-45% 25-35% Schedule conflicts, hunger management
Plant-based 45-55% 30-40% Social dining, nutrient planning
Important: These numbers reflect people maintaining strict adherence. Many people find success with modified versions or cycling between approaches.

Common Myths About Long-term Success

Myth 1: "If it worked short-term, it will work long-term"

Reality: Short-term results often come from initial motivation and dramatic changes. Long-term success requires different strategies—flexibility, social support, and realistic expectations.

Myth 2: "Successful people never struggle or slip up"

Reality: Studies show that people who maintain long-term changes actually experience regular setbacks. The difference is how quickly they get back on track.

Patient Story - Sarah, 3 years later: "I stopped trying to be perfect with keto after year one. Now I eat low-carb most of the time but have pizza with my kids on Fridays. I've maintained my 30-pound weight loss because I'm not stressed about being 'perfect.'"

Myth 3: "You need willpower to succeed long-term"

Reality: Willpower fades. Long-term success comes from building systems, changing your environment, and developing habits that don't require constant decision-making.

The Maintenance Phase: What Actually Works

After reviewing long-term studies, here are the strategies that consistently predict success:

  1. Flexible adherence - Following your plan 80% of the time rather than 100%
  2. Regular self-monitoring - Weekly weigh-ins or food tracking (not daily obsessing)
  3. Social support systems - Family involvement or community connections
  4. Problem-solving skills - Having plans for holidays, stress, travel
  5. Identity shift - Seeing yourself as "someone who eats well" rather than "someone on a diet"
Mindset Shift: Instead of asking "Can I do this forever?" ask "How can I adapt this to work with my real life?" The goal is progress, not perfection.

Your Long-term Success Action Plan

Phase 1: Assessment (Month 1-3)

  • Track what's working and what feels unsustainable
  • Identify your biggest challenges (social, practical, emotional)
  • Notice which changes feel natural vs. forced

Phase 2: Adaptation (Month 4-12)

  • Modify rules that create stress or isolation
  • Build in planned flexibility (like Sarah's Friday pizza)
  • Develop backup plans for common obstacles
  • Focus on maintaining 3-5 core habits rather than perfect adherence

Phase 3: Integration (Year 2+)

  • Regular check-ins with yourself or healthcare provider
  • Seasonal adjustments to your approach
  • Celebrating non-scale victories and health improvements
  • Helping others, which reinforces your own commitment
Patient Story - Mike, 5 years later: "I learned that 'long-term' doesn't mean doing the exact same thing forever. I do stricter low-carb in winter and more relaxed eating in summer when there's more social events. My health markers have stayed good, and I'm not burned out."

Red Flags: When to Reassess Your Approach

Consider changing your approach if you experience:
  • Constant thoughts about food or eating
  • Social isolation due to dietary restrictions
  • Guilt or shame around food choices
  • Physical symptoms (fatigue, mood changes, digestive issues)
  • All-or-nothing thinking ("I'm either perfect or a failure")

The Canadian Context: Making It Work Here

Consider these practical aspects of maintaining changes in Canada:

  • Seasonal challenges: Plan for comfort food cravings in winter, fresh produce availability
  • Food costs: Budget for higher costs of specialty foods, find affordable alternatives
  • Cultural diversity: Adapt traditional foods rather than eliminating them

Remember: The "best" diet is the one you can follow consistently while enjoying your life. Perfect adherence for 6 months followed by complete abandonment isn't better than good adherence for 6 years.
