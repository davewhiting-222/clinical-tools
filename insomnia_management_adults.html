Understanding and Managing Insomnia

Evidence-Based Strategies That Work Better Than Sleeping Pills

The Key Message

Insomnia is one of the most common health complaints — and one of the most treatable. The most effective treatment is not medication. It is a set of proven behavioural and cognitive strategies called CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia), recommended as the first-line treatment by every major medical guideline. These strategies produce better long-term results than sleeping pills, with no side effects.1,2,3

What Is Insomnia?

Insomnia is defined as difficulty falling asleep, difficulty staying asleep, or waking too early, combined with daytime consequences such as fatigue, difficulty concentrating, or mood disturbance. To be considered chronic insomnia, these symptoms must occur at least three nights per week for three months or more.1,4

Insomnia is remarkably common. Approximately 10–15% of adults meet criteria for chronic insomnia disorder, and up to 30–50% experience insomnia symptoms at some point in their lives. It is more common in women and tends to increase in prevalence with age, though it affects adults of all ages.4,5

An Important Distinction: Sleep Need Varies

There is no single "correct" amount of sleep. While most adults need approximately 7–9 hours, this varies considerably between individuals. Some people function well on 6 hours; others genuinely need 9. The key indicator of whether you're getting enough sleep is not a number on the clock — it's how you feel and function during the day. If you feel rested and alert, your sleep is likely adequate, regardless of the exact duration.6

Understanding How Sleep Works

Understanding the basic biology of sleep can help you work with your body rather than against it. Two key systems regulate your sleep:6,7

1. Sleep Pressure (the Homeostatic Drive)

From the moment you wake up, a chemical called adenosine gradually accumulates in your brain. The longer you've been awake, the more adenosine builds up, and the stronger your drive to sleep becomes. This is "sleep pressure." It's the reason you feel progressively sleepier as the day goes on, and it's why staying in bed for extended periods or napping during the day can actually make it harder to fall asleep at night — because these behaviours reduce the sleep pressure you've built up.6,7

2. The Circadian Clock (Your Body's Timer)

Your brain has an internal clock — the suprachiasmatic nucleus — that runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle. This clock regulates when you feel alert and when you feel sleepy, largely through the hormone melatonin. Bright light exposure (especially morning light) is the most powerful signal that keeps this clock synchronized. When your circadian rhythm is disrupted — by irregular schedules, shift work, or limited light exposure — sleep suffers.6,7

The Two Forces That Regulate Sleep

Left: Sleep pressure builds throughout the day. Napping or spending too long in bed reduces this pressure. Right: Your circadian rhythm creates natural peaks and dips in alertness, including the well-known "afternoon slump." Sleep comes most easily when high sleep pressure coincides with your circadian dip in alertness.6,7

What Keeps Insomnia Going?

Many things can trigger insomnia — stress, illness, a life change, shift work, pain, or a new baby. But what keeps insomnia going long after the initial trigger has resolved is usually a combination of behavioural habits and thought patterns that develop in response to poor sleep.8,9

The Insomnia Cycle: How Short-Term Sleep Problems Become Chronic

A stressful event triggers poor sleep. The worry and compensating behaviours that follow create a self-reinforcing cycle that keeps insomnia going even after the original trigger is gone. CBT-I targets each component of this cycle.8,9

When to See Your Doctor

Before focusing on behavioural strategies, it's worth checking whether a treatable medical condition is contributing to your sleep difficulties. See your healthcare provider if:4,10

  • Your sleep problems have persisted for more than three months
  • You experience loud snoring, gasping, or breathing pauses during sleep — these may indicate obstructive sleep apnea, which is common and highly treatable
  • You have uncomfortable sensations in your legs when resting, or a compelling urge to move them (restless legs syndrome)
  • You experience excessive daytime sleepiness that interferes with work, driving, or daily activities
  • Your insomnia coincides with significant mood changes, as depression and anxiety both disrupt sleep and may need specific treatment

Several common medical conditions and medications can contribute to or worsen insomnia:4,10,11

Treating an underlying medical cause is often more effective than treating the insomnia directly. If you suspect a contributing factor, discuss it with your doctor before focusing solely on behavioural strategies.4,10

The Role of Your Thoughts About Sleep

One of the most powerful — and often overlooked — factors in insomnia is how you think about sleep. This isn't about telling you that the problem is "all in your head." Sleep difficulties are real, and the frustration they cause is completely understandable. However, research consistently shows that our thoughts and beliefs about sleep can either help us sleep better or keep us trapped in a cycle of worsening insomnia.8,12,13

A Common and Understandable Pattern

After a few poor nights, it's perfectly natural to start worrying. You might think: "If I don't get 8 hours tonight, I won't be able to cope tomorrow." This kind of thinking is completely understandable — but it actually makes sleep harder, not easier. The worry activates your stress response (cortisol, adrenaline, muscle tension), which is the opposite of what your brain needs to fall asleep.8,12

This creates a frustrating cycle: poor sleep → worry about sleep → stress response → even worse sleep. The good news is that this cycle can be broken — and the strategies in this handout are specifically designed to do that.

Thought Patterns That Can Worsen Insomnia

Certain thought patterns, while completely understandable, can amplify sleep difficulties. Recognizing these patterns is the first step to changing them:12,13,14

  • Predicting the worst: "I won't be able to function at all tomorrow" — In reality, most people manage reasonably well after a poor night, even if it doesn't feel great
  • Overgeneralizing: "I never sleep well anymore" — Looking at a sleep diary often reveals that some nights are actually better than others
  • Rigid expectations: "I absolutely must get 8 hours" — Sleep needs vary, and placing rigid demands on sleep creates pressure that paradoxically makes sleep harder
  • Monitoring and calculating: Constantly checking the clock and calculating remaining sleep hours increases alertness when you most need to relax
  • Blaming everything on sleep: "I feel lousy because I didn't sleep" — While sleep matters, other factors like nutrition, activity, mood, and overall health also influence how you feel

Reframing Your Thinking

Reframing doesn't mean pretending everything is fine — it means finding a more balanced and accurate perspective. Here are some examples:12,13,14

"I have to fall asleep right now or tomorrow will be a disaster."

CBT-I: The Gold Standard Treatment for Insomnia

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults by the American College of Physicians, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, the European Sleep Research Society, and the Canadian Sleep Society. It is more effective than sleeping pills in the long run and has no side effects.1,2,3

CBT-I works by addressing the behavioural and thinking patterns that perpetuate insomnia. It typically involves 4–8 sessions, but many people see improvement within 2–4 weeks. The five core techniques are described below.1,2

Technique 1: Sleep Restriction Therapy

What It Is

This is the most powerful component of CBT-I, despite sounding counterintuitive. Sleep restriction temporarily limits the time you spend in bed to match the time you're actually sleeping. This builds up strong sleep pressure, making sleep deeper and more consolidated.1,2,15

How It Works

  1. Track your sleep for 1–2 weeks using the sleep diary below. Calculate your average total sleep time. For example, if you're in bed for 9 hours but only sleeping about 6 hours, your total sleep time is 6 hours.
  2. Set your "sleep window" to match your total sleep time (minimum 5 hours). Choose a fixed wake-up time and count backward. If your average sleep time is 6 hours and you want to wake at 6:30 AM, your bedtime would be 12:30 AM.
  3. Get up at the same time every day — including weekends. This is critical for resetting your body clock.
  4. Don't go to bed until your scheduled bedtime, even if you feel sleepy earlier.
  5. Gradually expand your sleep window by 15–30 minutes once you're sleeping for 85–90% of the time you're in bed (this is called "sleep efficiency"). Continue expanding until you reach a satisfying amount of sleep.

Why It Works

By spending less time lying awake in bed, you build strong sleep pressure and retrain your brain to associate the bed with sleep rather than wakefulness. Most people find their sleep becomes deeper and more satisfying within 2–3 weeks, even though the first week can be challenging.1,15

Important Note on Sleep Restriction

Sleep restriction can cause temporary daytime sleepiness in the first 1–2 weeks. If you have a history of seizures or bipolar disorder, discuss this technique with your healthcare provider before starting. If you drive or operate machinery, be especially cautious during the initial adjustment period. Do not reduce total time in bed below 5 hours.2,15

Technique 2: Stimulus Control

What It Is

Stimulus control re-establishes the mental connection between your bed and sleep. If you've spent many hours lying awake in bed, your brain may have learned to associate the bed with wakefulness, frustration, and worry rather than with sleep. Stimulus control reverses this.1,2,16

The Rules

  1. Use the bed only for sleep and intimacy. No reading, watching TV, scrolling your phone, eating, or working in bed.
  2. Go to bed only when you're sleepy — not just because it's "bedtime." Sleepiness means heavy eyelids, yawning, and difficulty staying awake — not just feeling tired.
  3. If you can't fall asleep within roughly 20 minutes (don't watch the clock — estimate), get out of bed and go to another room. Do something quiet and relaxing in dim light: read a book, listen to calm music, do a puzzle. Return to bed only when you feel sleepy again.
  4. Repeat step 3 as many times as needed during the night. This may happen several times in the first week — that's completely normal and expected.
  5. Wake at the same time every morning regardless of how much you slept.
  6. Avoid napping, or limit naps to 20–30 minutes before 2:00 PM.

Why It Works

Your brain learns through association. By consistently pairing the bed with sleep (and getting out when you're not sleeping), you retrain your brain to "switch off" when you lie down. This is one of the most researched and effective techniques in sleep medicine.1,16

Technique 3: Sleep Hygiene

What It Is

Sleep hygiene refers to environmental and behavioural practices that support good sleep. On its own, sleep hygiene is usually not enough to fix insomnia — but combined with the techniques above, it creates optimal conditions for sleep.1,2

Environmental Factors

  • Keep your bedroom cool (18–20°C / 65–68°F). Your core body temperature needs to drop for sleep onset — a cool room helps this happen.
  • Make it dark. Use blackout curtains or a sleep mask. Even small amounts of light can suppress melatonin production and disrupt your circadian clock.
  • Minimize noise. Use earplugs or a white noise machine if needed.
  • Reserve the bedroom for sleep. If possible, avoid working, eating, or spending leisure time in the bedroom.

Behavioural Factors

  • Limit caffeine after noon. Caffeine's half-life is 5–7 hours, meaning half the caffeine from an afternoon coffee is still active in your brain at bedtime. Some people metabolize caffeine more slowly and may need to stop even earlier.
  • Limit alcohol. While alcohol may help you fall asleep faster, it significantly disrupts the second half of the night — causing fragmented, non-restorative sleep, increased awakenings, and suppressed REM sleep.
  • Avoid large meals within 2–3 hours of bedtime. A small snack is fine if hunger is an issue.
  • Limit screen time before bed. The blue light from phones, tablets, and computers suppresses melatonin. If you must use screens in the evening, use blue-light filtering settings and dim the brightness.
  • Avoid nicotine close to bedtime. Nicotine is a stimulant that increases arousal and can delay sleep onset.

Technique 4: Relaxation Training

What It Is

Relaxation techniques reduce the physical tension and mental arousal that can prevent sleep. These are skills that improve with practice — don't expect them to work perfectly the first time.1,2,17

Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)

This technique involves systematically tensing and then releasing muscle groups throughout your body:17

  1. Lie down comfortably and take a few slow, deep breaths.
  2. Starting with your feet, tense the muscles firmly (but not painfully) for 5–10 seconds.
  3. Release suddenly and notice the contrast between tension and relaxation. Rest for 15–20 seconds.
  4. Move upward through your body: calves → thighs → abdomen → chest → hands → arms → shoulders → neck → face.
  5. After completing all muscle groups, take a few minutes to enjoy the feeling of whole-body relaxation.

Practice PMR daily, ideally both during the day and at bedtime. Many people find it becomes noticeably more effective after 1–2 weeks of regular practice.

Diaphragmatic (Belly) Breathing

Slow, deep breathing activates your parasympathetic nervous system — the "rest and digest" system that counteracts the stress response:17

  1. Place one hand on your chest and one on your belly.
  2. Breathe in slowly through your nose for 4 seconds, allowing your belly (not your chest) to rise.
  3. Pause briefly.
  4. Exhale slowly through your mouth for 6 seconds, letting your belly fall.
  5. Repeat for 5–10 minutes.

The Body Scan

A gentler alternative to PMR:17

  1. Lie comfortably and close your eyes.
  2. Slowly direct your attention to each part of your body, starting at your toes.
  3. Simply notice any sensations — warmth, tingling, tension — without trying to change them.
  4. Imagine each body part becoming heavy and relaxed as your attention moves through it.
  5. Move slowly from toes to head over 10–20 minutes.

Technique 5: Cognitive Restructuring

What It Is

This is the structured approach to identifying and reframing the unhelpful thoughts about sleep discussed earlier. It helps you develop a more realistic and less stressful relationship with sleep.12,13,14

How to Practice

  1. Notice the thought. When you catch yourself thinking something stressful about sleep, pause and identify the specific thought. Write it down if you can.
  2. Examine the evidence. Ask yourself: Is this thought completely true? What is the actual evidence? Have I managed okay after a poor night before?
  3. Consider alternatives. What would I say to a friend who had this same worry? What's a more balanced way to look at this?
  4. Replace with a balanced thought. Develop a realistic alternative that acknowledges the difficulty without exaggerating it.

Helpful Reminders

  • One poor night of sleep does not cause lasting harm — your body has powerful recovery mechanisms
  • Quiet rest (even without sleep) provides significant physical restoration
  • People routinely underestimate how much they actually slept — research shows insomnia sufferers often sleep more than they think18
  • Sleep drive is one of the most powerful biological drives — if you're tired enough, your body will sleep
  • Trying harder to sleep is counterproductive — sleep comes most easily when you stop chasing it

Building a Wind-Down Routine

A consistent pre-sleep routine signals to your brain that it's time to transition from wakefulness to sleep. Think of it as a "landing approach" — gradually reducing stimulation over 30–60 minutes before bed.1,2

Sample Wind-Down Routine

60 minutes before bed: Turn off screens. Dim the lights in your home. Switch to relaxing activities.

45 minutes before bed: Have a warm bath or shower. The subsequent drop in body temperature as you cool down promotes sleepiness.

30 minutes before bed: Light reading, gentle stretching, or listening to calm music or a podcast.

15 minutes before bed: Practice your relaxation technique (PMR, breathing, or body scan).

Bedtime: Lights off only when you feel genuinely sleepy.

The Importance of Daytime Habits

What you do during the day has a profound effect on how you sleep at night. These daytime strategies are just as important as what you do at bedtime:6,7,19

Morning Light Exposure

Bright light exposure — especially in the morning — is one of the most powerful tools for regulating your circadian rhythm. Aim for 30 minutes of bright outdoor light within 1–2 hours of waking. Even on a cloudy day, outdoor light is many times brighter than indoor lighting. This helps anchor your internal clock and promotes natural melatonin release in the evening.6,7

Physical Activity

Regular exercise is one of the most evidence-supported strategies for improving sleep quality. Key points:19,20

  • Even 20–30 minutes of moderate activity most days makes a meaningful difference
  • Morning or afternoon exercise is ideal; vigorous exercise within 2–3 hours of bedtime may delay sleep onset in some people
  • The sleep benefit of exercise accumulates over weeks — keep at it even if you don't notice immediate improvement
  • Any movement counts — walking, cycling, swimming, yoga, gardening

Napping Guidelines

Napping is a common response to poor nighttime sleep, but it reduces the sleep pressure you need to fall asleep at night. If you do nap:6,7

  • Keep naps to 20–30 minutes maximum
  • Nap before 2:00 PM only
  • If you're trying to implement sleep restriction therapy, avoid naps entirely during the initial phase

Why Medication Is Not the First Choice

It's natural to want a quick solution for sleep problems, and sleeping pills may seem like the obvious answer. However, for most adults with chronic insomnia, the risks and limitations of sleep medications mean they are not recommended as the first approach:2,3,21,22

Limitations and Risks of Sleep Medications

Benzodiazepines and "Z-drugs" (zopiclone, zolpidem) are the most commonly prescribed sleep medications. Here is what the evidence shows:21,22,23

  • Modest actual benefit: On average, sleeping pills add only about 20–30 minutes of sleep per night — far less than most people expect.
  • Dependence and tolerance: The body adapts to these medications within weeks, requiring higher doses for the same effect. Stopping them after regular use often causes withdrawal symptoms and rebound insomnia.
  • Next-day impairment: These medications commonly cause residual morning drowsiness, impaired coordination, and reduced alertness — which can affect driving, work performance, and safety.
  • Cognitive effects: Impaired memory formation during the medication's active period is well-documented. Concerns about long-term cognitive effects with prolonged use are the subject of ongoing research.
  • No treatment of the underlying problem: Sleeping pills suppress symptoms while you take them but do not address the behavioural and cognitive patterns that maintain insomnia. When the medication is stopped, the insomnia typically returns.

Both Choosing Wisely Canada and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommend CBT-I — not medication — as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults.2,3,23

CBT-I, by contrast, produces equal or better short-term results and superior long-term outcomes compared to medication — without any of these risks. The effects of CBT-I are durable, meaning they last long after treatment ends, while the effects of medication stop when you stop taking them.1,2,3

If You're Currently Taking Sleep Medication

If you're already using sleep medication, do not stop it abruptly. Work with your healthcare provider to develop a gradual tapering plan, ideally while simultaneously implementing CBT-I techniques. Many people successfully reduce or eliminate sleep medication while actually sleeping better through behavioural strategies.2,22

Accessing CBT-I

CBT-I can be delivered in several ways:1,2,3

  • In-person with a trained therapist: Psychologists or therapists trained in CBT-I offer individual or group sessions (typically 4–8 sessions). Ask your healthcare provider for a referral.
  • Digital CBT-I programs: App-based programs such as Insomnia Coach (free, from the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs) and HALEO (available to some Canadian residents) offer structured CBT-I guidance and have been shown to be effective in clinical trials.
  • Self-directed: Using the techniques in this handout consistently for 4–6 weeks. While working with a therapist is ideal, self-directed CBT-I can still produce meaningful improvements.

Putting It All Together

Improving sleep is a process, not an overnight fix. Here's a practical roadmap:1,2

Your 6-Week Plan

1 Weeks 1–2: Assess and Prepare

  • Keep a sleep diary to establish your baseline
  • Implement sleep hygiene changes (environment, caffeine, alcohol, screens)
  • Begin practicing a relaxation technique daily
  • Address any medical factors with your healthcare provider

2 Weeks 3–4: Core CBT-I Techniques

  • Start stimulus control (bed = sleep only; get up if awake)
  • Begin sleep restriction based on your diary data
  • Practice cognitive restructuring when you notice unhelpful thoughts
  • Maintain your wind-down routine

3 Weeks 5–6: Adjust and Consolidate

  • Gradually expand your sleep window as sleep efficiency improves above 85%
  • Continue refining your routine based on what works for you
  • Review your sleep diary for trends and improvements
  • Discuss progress with your healthcare provider

Be patient with yourself. Most people notice meaningful improvements by weeks 3–4. Some techniques, especially sleep restriction, may feel harder before they feel better. This is normal — stick with the plan.

The Bottom Line

Insomnia is common, treatable, and not something you simply have to live with. The most effective treatment is not a pill — it's the proven behavioural and cognitive strategies of CBT-I, which address the root causes of insomnia rather than masking the symptoms.1,2,3

The path to better sleep involves understanding how sleep works, changing the habits that keep insomnia going, being kind to yourself about the process, and working with your body's natural rhythms rather than fighting them. Small, consistent changes really do add up to meaningful improvement.

A Final Thought

Your body has been falling asleep successfully every night of your life. It hasn't forgotten how. Sometimes it just needs a bit of help getting out of its own way. By reducing the pressure you put on sleep and creating the right conditions, you're giving your body the best chance to do what it already knows how to do.
