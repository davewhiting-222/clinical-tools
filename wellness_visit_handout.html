Getting the Most Out of Your Health Visit

Quality Over Quantity: Evidence-Based Care That Works

We're On the Same Team

You want to stay healthy. We want to help you stay healthy. This handout explains how modern medicine has moved beyond the traditional "annual physical" to a more effective, personalized approach based on scientific evidence. Our goal isn't to do less for you—it's to do what actually works best for you.

The Evolution of the "Annual Physical"

The Old Way The Evidence-Based Way
✗ Everyone gets the same tests once a year, regardless of symptoms or risk factors ✓ Testing is personalized based on your age, family history, symptoms, and risk factors
✗ "Fishing expedition" blood work looking for any abnormality ✓ Targeted screening for conditions where early detection makes a difference
✗ Focus on finding disease ✓ Focus on preventing disease through proven interventions
✗ "More testing is better" ✓ "The right testing is better"

For decades, patients were told to come in once a year for a comprehensive head-to-toe examination and a general panel of blood work, regardless of how they felt. The thinking was simple: catching problems early must be good.1,2

But medicine has advanced. We now follow evidence-based guidelines (such as "Choosing Wisely" from medical specialty societies) that focus on proven prevention rather than generic testing. Multiple large studies have shown that routine annual physicals with comprehensive screening don't improve health outcomes and may even cause harm through unnecessary follow-up testing and procedures.1,2,3

Why "More Testing" Isn't Always "Better Health"

The Problem with "Fishing Expeditions"

When we order tests without a specific reason—just hoping to find something—we often create confusion rather than clarity. Here's why:4,5

Understanding False Positives: When Tests Lie

What is a false positive? Lab tests aren't perfect. Sometimes a test says something is wrong when you are actually perfectly healthy. This happens because:4,5

  • Labs define "normal" as the middle 95% of healthy people—meaning 5% of perfectly healthy people will have "abnormal" results by definition
  • When you run 20 different tests, the chances of at least one false positive exceed 60%
  • Rare diseases are, by definition, rare—so most "positive" tests for rare conditions in healthy people are false alarms
The Cascade of Care: How One False Positive Can Snowball

This "cascade of care" is a well-documented phenomenon in medicine. A single unnecessary test can trigger a chain reaction of interventions, each carrying its own risks, ultimately harming a person who was healthy to begin with.4,5,6

Our Guiding Principle

"First, do no harm." We want to protect you from the harm of unnecessary medical intervention just as much as we want to protect you from disease. Sometimes the best medicine is no medicine.3,6

Two Common Scenarios: Which One Are You?

Scenario A: Do You Have a Specific Worry?

Many patients book a "physical" because they're experiencing a specific symptom—fatigue, vague pain, difficulty sleeping, sexual dysfunction—but feel awkward bringing it up directly. They hope a general blood test will "find it."7

The Reality: General Panels Often Miss Specific Problems

A standard "wellness panel" isn't designed to diagnose specific ailments. If you're experiencing symptoms, we need to know about them so we can order the right tests, not just any tests.7,8

The Better Strategy: Be Direct

  • If you're tired, tell us you're tired. We'll investigate thyroid function, anemia, sleep disorders, depression, and other specific causes.
  • If you have pain, tell us where. We'll examine that area and order appropriate imaging or tests.
  • If something feels "off," describe it. Vague symptoms need thoughtful investigation, not random testing.

Key Takeaway: Help us be medical detectives. Give us the clues (your symptoms) so we can solve the mystery efficiently and accurately.

Understanding Different Types of Visits

Diagnostic Visit: You have symptoms. We investigate the cause with targeted testing.
Screening Visit: You feel fine. We look for hidden diseases where early detection is proven to help.8

These require different approaches. Mixing them up leads to confusion and poor care.

Scenario B: Looking for Reassurance?

Perhaps you feel fine, but a family member recently got sick, or you just want to make sure you're "okay." This is completely valid—but let's make sure we screen for the right things.9,10

What We DO Screen For: The High-Value List

These are conditions where scientific evidence clearly shows that early detection improves outcomes and saves lives:9,10,11

Blood Pressure

Screening for hypertension prevents strokes and heart attacks. Everyone should be screened regularly.

Diabetes (Type 2)

Screening in adults age 35-70 who are overweight or have risk factors. Early detection allows lifestyle changes.

Cholesterol

Screening for cardiovascular risk assessment, particularly if you have other risk factors.

Colon Cancer

Starting at age 45-50 (earlier with family history). Multiple effective screening options available.

Breast Cancer

Mammography for women starting at age 40-50, continuing to age 75 (timing depends on individual risk).

Cervical Cancer

Pap smears starting at age 21, with age-appropriate intervals based on guidelines.

Osteoporosis

Bone density screening for women 65+ (earlier if risk factors present).

Immunizations

Prevention is the best medicine. Keeping vaccines up-to-date prevents serious diseases.

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm

One-time ultrasound for men age 65-75 who have ever smoked.

Lung Cancer

Low-dose CT screening for adults 50-80 with significant smoking history.

Hepatitis C

One-time screening for adults born 1945-1965 and others at risk.

Depression

Screening with validated questionnaires in all adults, with systems in place for treatment.

Customized Risk Assessment

This is where family history matters. If your parent had a heart attack at age 45, your screening strategy changes. If you have a strong family history of certain cancers, we may start screening earlier.9,10

We don't use a cookie-cutter checklist. We look at your specific genetics, lifestyle, and personal circumstances to decide what tests add value to your life.

Preparing for Your Visit: A Partnership Approach

Reframing the "Periodic Wellness Visit"

Think of this appointment as a time to:8,11

  • Update your medical and family history
  • Discuss lifestyle factors (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol, stress)
  • Review and update preventative screenings based on your age and risk factors
  • Address specific concerns or symptoms you're experiencing
  • Ensure your immunizations are current
  • Review medications and ensure they're still appropriate

Your Checklist for a Productive Visit

1
Be Specific About Symptoms If you have a symptom, write it down before your visit. Include: When did it start? How often does it happen? What makes it better or worse? Mention it first—don't wait to see if tests will "catch it."
2
Update Your Family History Has anyone in your family developed a new condition since your last visit? Particularly: heart disease before age 60, cancers (especially breast, colon, ovarian), diabetes, or sudden death.
3
List Medications and Supplements Bring a list of everything you take, including over-the-counter medications, vitamins, and herbal supplements. Note any side effects or concerns.
4
Discuss Lifestyle Honestly We need to know about your diet, exercise habits, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, drug use, and stress levels. We're not here to judge—we're here to help.
5
Ask Questions If we don't recommend a test you expected, ask why. If we do recommend a test, ask what we're looking for and what we'll do with the results. Understanding the reasoning helps you make informed decisions.
6
Trust the Evidence If your doctor recommends against a test, it's because medical evidence suggests the risks outweigh the benefits for you right now. This doesn't mean we don't care—it means we care enough to protect you from unnecessary harm.

Common Questions and Concerns

"But what if you miss something?"

This is a valid concern. However, research consistently shows that routine screening tests in healthy people without risk factors are more likely to cause harm than benefit. The chance of missing a significant disease is far lower than the chance of generating false positives that lead to unnecessary procedures.1,2,3,5

"My friend's doctor orders lots of tests. Why don't you?"

Medicine is evolving rapidly. What was standard practice 20 years ago may no longer be considered best practice today. We follow evidence-based guidelines developed by expert panels who have reviewed thousands of studies. More testing isn't better care—appropriate testing is better care.3,6

"Can I still request a test if I'm worried?"

Absolutely. After we discuss the potential benefits and harms, if you still feel strongly about a test, we can work together to decide what's best. Shared decision-making is at the heart of good medical care.11

What About Online/Commercial Testing?

Be Cautious with Direct-to-Consumer Testing

Many companies now offer comprehensive blood panels, genetic testing, or full-body scans directly to consumers. While these may seem appealing, they come with significant concerns:12,13

  • High rate of false positives leading to unnecessary anxiety and follow-up
  • No medical context for interpreting results
  • May detect "incidentalomas"—findings that would never have caused problems but now require expensive workup
  • Not subject to the same quality standards as medical testing

If you've already done commercial testing, bring the results to your appointment so we can review them together and determine if follow-up is truly needed.

The Bottom Line: Quality Over Quantity

Our Commitment to You

We are 100% committed to your long-term health. That commitment means:

  • Ordering tests that are proven to improve outcomes
  • Protecting you from the harms of unnecessary medical procedures
  • Personalizing your care based on your unique risk factors
  • Being honest about what we know and what we don't know
  • Listening to your concerns and addressing them thoughtfully
  • Spending our time together on what truly matters for your health

We're on the same team. Let's focus on the tests and treatments that truly make a difference in your life.

Remember

The goal of modern healthcare isn't to find something wrong with you—it's to keep you healthy. Sometimes that means doing less testing and focusing more on the fundamentals: nutrition, exercise, sleep, stress management, social connections, and evidence-based preventive care.

Your health is too important to waste on tests that don't help. Let's work together to focus on what really matters.
