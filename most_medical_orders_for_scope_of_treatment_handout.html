Planning Your Care: Understanding MOST

Prepared by your family doctor August 11, 2026

What Is a MOST Form?

MOST stands for Medical Orders for Scope of Treatment. It is an official medical document — signed by you and your doctor or nurse practitioner — that records the level of medical care you would want to receive if you became seriously ill or had a medical emergency and could not speak for yourself.

Think of it like a detailed instruction sheet for your healthcare team. It takes the guesswork out of a very stressful moment and makes sure that your wishes are respected.

📋 Why this matters

Without a MOST form, emergency teams are required by law to do everything possible to keep you alive — including measures you may not want. A MOST form gives you a voice even when you cannot speak.

⚠️ Important — this worksheet is not a legal order

This handout is a planning tool to help you think about your wishes and prepare for a conversation with your doctor or nurse practitioner. Only the official MOST form — discussed, agreed upon, and signed together with your healthcare provider — is legally valid.

Who Should Have a MOST Form?

A MOST form is most useful for people who:

  • Have a serious or life-limiting illness (such as advanced cancer, heart failure, or COPD)
  • Are elderly or frail
  • Live in a care home or receive home support
  • Have strong feelings about the kind of care they do — or do not — want

It is never too early to have this conversation. Many people find it gives them peace of mind, not anxiety.

💬 A note on this conversation

Talking about serious illness and end-of-life care can feel uncomfortable at first. That is completely normal. Your doctor is here to guide you — there are no right or wrong answers. This is about your values and your life.

How the MOST Form Works

The MOST form has two main parts:

  1. Part 1 — Resuscitation Status & General Medical Care: This is the big-picture choice. It covers whether you would want CPR (chest compressions), electrical shocks to the heart (defibrillation), or a breathing tube (intubation), and the overall level of care you receive.
  2. Part 2 — Specific Medical Interventions: This covers individual treatments such as blood transfusions, kidney dialysis, and feeding tubes.

Part 1 — Your Care Category Explained

There are six categories, grouped into Comfort & Supportive Care (M categories) and Critical Care (C categories).

Comfort & Supportive Care — M Categories

These categories focus on quality of life, comfort, and dignity. They do not include CPR (chest compressions), electrical shocks to the heart (defibrillation), or a breathing tube (intubation).

Category What it means in plain language
M1 Comfort & Dignity Only. Your care team focuses entirely on keeping you comfortable, managing pain, and supporting you emotionally and spiritually. You will not be moved to a hospital unless your comfort needs cannot be met where you are. This is chosen when someone is nearing the end of life and wants a peaceful, natural death.
M2 Active Medical Care — Stay Where You Are. Treatments that might cure or control your illness (for example, antibiotics or IV fluids) are given where you currently live or are being cared for. You would only be moved to a hospital if those treatments truly cannot be given in your current location. No ICU, CPR, defibrillation, or breathing tube.
M3 Active Medical Care — Including Hospital Transfer. You receive treatments aimed at curing or managing your illness, and you agree to be transferred to a hospital or higher-level care if needed for tests or treatment. No ICU, CPR, defibrillation, or breathing tube.

Critical Care — C Categories

These categories are for people who want full medical investigations and treatments in an intensive care unit (ICU) setting, with varying levels of life support.

Category What it means in plain language
C0 ICU Care — No CPR, No Defibrillation, No Breathing Tube. You receive intensive monitoring and specialist medications in the ICU, but your team would not perform CPR (chest compressions), give electrical shocks to the heart, or insert a breathing tube.
C1 ICU Care Including Breathing Tube — No CPR or Defibrillation. In addition to ICU care, a breathing tube and mechanical ventilator (breathing machine) can be used. However, your team would not perform CPR or use electrical shocks to the heart.
C2 Full Life Support — Everything Medically Appropriate. Your team uses all available critical care interventions, including CPR, electrical shocks to the heart (defibrillation), and a breathing tube (intubation). This is often called "full code."
✅ There is no wrong choice

Choosing M1 is not "giving up." Choosing C2 is not "being unrealistic." The right choice is the one that matches your values and goals. Your doctor will help you understand which options are medically reasonable for your individual situation.

Part 2 — Specific Treatments

Depending on your overall care goals, you can also say whether you would or would not want specific medical treatments. Here is a plain-language description of each one:

Treatment What it means
Blood / Blood Products A transfusion of blood cells, plasma, or platelets — for example, if you are bleeding heavily or your blood count drops dangerously low.
Nutritional Support Receiving nutrition through a tube inserted into your stomach (tube feeding) or directly into a vein (IV nutrition) when you cannot eat on your own.
Dialysis A machine that cleans your blood when your kidneys stop working well enough to do the job on their own. Sessions are usually several hours, several times a week.
Non-Invasive Ventilation Breathing support given through a tightly fitting face mask — for example, CPAP or BiPAP. This helps your breathing without requiring a tube to be inserted into your airway.

What Happens Next?

Here is what to expect after you have thought through your preferences:

  1. Bring this worksheet to your appointment. Your doctor will review your choices with you and answer any questions.
  2. Have an honest conversation. Your doctor will let you know which options are medically appropriate for your situation and help you think through any trade-offs.
  3. The official MOST form is completed and signed. Both you and your healthcare provider sign it. It becomes a legal medical order.
  4. Keep a copy somewhere accessible. Make sure family members or your substitute decision-maker (the person legally allowed to make decisions for you if you cannot) know where it is.
  5. Review it if things change. Your MOST form can be updated at any time if your health situation or wishes change.
👪 Tell someone you trust

Let a family member, close friend, or your substitute decision-maker (sometimes called a personal directive or healthcare proxy in other provinces) know about your wishes — even before the form is signed. In BC, this person is often named in a Representation Agreement.

✅ Your choices can change

You are never locked in. A MOST form can be reviewed, changed, or cancelled at any time — as long as you have the ability to make your own decisions (called decision-making capacity). Your doctor will reassess it regularly with you.

Common Questions

Does choosing comfort care mean my doctor will give up on me?

Not at all. Comfort care is active, expert medical care — it simply focuses on quality of life, pain management, and your dignity rather than aggressive life-prolonging treatments. Many people live well for months or years on comfort-focused care.

What if I change my mind in an emergency?

If you are conscious and able to make decisions, you can always change your mind in the moment. Your healthcare team will always listen to you first.

Can my family override my MOST form?

No. Your MOST form reflects your documented wishes. Family members cannot overrule it. This is one of the most important reasons to have the conversation with family ahead of time — so there are no surprises.
