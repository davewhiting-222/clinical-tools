Tinea Versicolor

Understanding and Treating This Common Fungal Skin Condition

The Most Important Message

Tinea versicolor is a common, harmless fungal condition caused by yeast that normally lives on everyone's skin. It is not contagious, not caused by poor hygiene, and not dangerous. Most cases respond well to simple over-the-counter treatments available at any pharmacy. The main challenge is that skin colour changes can take weeks to months to normalize after the yeast is cleared — this is normal and does not mean the treatment has failed.

What Is Tinea Versicolor?

Tinea versicolor (also called pityriasis versicolor) is a superficial fungal infection of the skin caused by an overgrowth of yeast belonging to the genus Malassezia — most commonly Malassezia globosa and Malassezia furfur.1,2 Despite its name, "tinea" versicolor is not related to ringworm or athlete's foot. It is caused by a completely different type of organism.

Malassezia is part of the normal microbiome of human skin — virtually everyone carries it without any problems. Tinea versicolor develops when this yeast transitions from its harmless budding form into a filamentous (thread-like) form that colonizes the outermost layer of the skin and disrupts normal pigmentation. This shift is driven by specific environmental and individual conditions, not by contact with an infected person.1,2,3

What Causes the Overgrowth?

Several factors encourage Malassezia to overgrow and become problematic:2,3,4

  • Heat and humidity: The most important triggers. The condition is far more common in warm, humid climates and flares during summer months.
  • Sweating: Heavy sweating from exercise, physical work, or hot weather creates a warm, moist skin environment that favours yeast growth.
  • Oily skin: Malassezia feeds on the fatty acids in skin oil (sebum). People with naturally oilier skin are more susceptible.
  • Adolescence and young adulthood: Oil glands are most active during these years, explaining why tinea versicolor is most common between ages 15 and 35.
  • Immunosuppression: Weakened immune function — from illness, medications, or stress — increases susceptibility.
  • Corticosteroid use: Both oral and topical steroid medications can promote yeast overgrowth.
  • Genetic susceptibility: Some people are simply more prone to recurrence, regardless of other factors.

Not Related to Hygiene

Tinea versicolor has nothing to do with cleanliness. It occurs equally in people who shower regularly and maintain excellent hygiene. Because Malassezia is a normal skin resident, you cannot "catch" tinea versicolor from another person, and there is no need to avoid contact with others — this condition is not contagious.

How Does It Look and Feel?

The defining feature of tinea versicolor is patches of skin that are a different colour from the surrounding skin — hence "versicolor," meaning varied colours. The exact appearance depends on your natural skin tone and sun exposure:1,2,5

  • Hypopigmented (lighter) patches: The most common appearance, especially on darker skin tones or tanned skin. The yeast produces compounds that block melanin production, leaving pale or near-white areas.
  • Hyperpigmented (darker) patches: More often seen on lighter skin tones, appearing as tan, pink, or light brown patches.
  • Pink or salmon-coloured patches: Often the first sign noticed, particularly in winter when skin is not tanned.

Other typical features include:1,2,5

  • Patches ranging from a few millimetres to several centimetres, sometimes merging into larger areas
  • A fine, powdery scale on the surface — most visible when the patch is gently scratched
  • Most commonly found on the upper trunk (back, chest, shoulders), upper arms, and neck — areas where oil glands are most concentrated
  • The face is less commonly affected in adults, though more so in adolescents
  • Mild itching, especially in warm weather — though many people experience no itch at all
  • Patches do not tan normally with sun exposure, which is when the condition is often first noticed as pale spots appear against tanned skin
Where Tinea Versicolor Typically Appears

Tinea versicolor most commonly affects the upper trunk, shoulders, neck, and upper arms — areas with the highest density of oil-producing glands. The back is equally or more commonly involved than the front.

How Is It Diagnosed?

Tinea versicolor is usually diagnosed on clinical appearance alone by your doctor. When the diagnosis is uncertain, two simple in-office tests can help confirm it:1,5,6

  • Wood's lamp (ultraviolet light): In a darkened room, the yeast on affected skin often fluoresces a yellow-green or copper-orange colour under UV light.
  • KOH (potassium hydroxide) skin scraping: A gentle scraping of scale from the patch is examined under a microscope. The characteristic appearance — clusters of round yeast cells alongside short, curved filaments — is often described as "spaghetti and meatballs."

Skin biopsies or blood tests are not required for diagnosis.

Treatment: Starting Simple

Treatment works by reducing the yeast population on the skin. Most cases of tinea versicolor can be treated successfully with products available without a prescription. Prescription medications are reserved for widespread or stubborn cases. The approach below moves from simplest to more involved.

Step 1: Over-the-Counter Antifungal Shampoos Used as a Body Wash

The most effective and cost-efficient first-line treatment uses antifungal shampoos — widely available at pharmacies — applied to the affected areas of the body and left on briefly before rinsing. These products are not used on the scalp for this purpose; they are applied to the trunk and other affected skin.7,8

First Choice: Selenium Sulfide or Ketoconazole Shampoo

Products to look for:

  • Selenium sulfide 1% shampoo (e.g., Selsun Blue, Head & Shoulders Clinical Strength, and generics) — available OTC
  • Ketoconazole 1% shampoo (e.g., Nizoral A-D) — available OTC; 2% is prescription strength

How to use: Apply the shampoo to dry or slightly damp skin on all affected areas (typically the chest, back, shoulders, upper arms, and neck). Leave it on for 10 minutes, then rinse off completely. Repeat daily for 2 weeks. Some guidelines also support a single 24-hour application of selenium sulfide left on overnight, though daily 10-minute applications are better tolerated.

Cure rates with this approach are reported at 70 to 90% in clinical studies.7,8

Step 2: Over-the-Counter Topical Antifungal Creams

Standard antifungal creams used for athlete's foot and jock itch are also effective for tinea versicolor. These azole-based creams are particularly useful when the affected area is small or localized.9

  • Clotrimazole 1% cream (e.g., Canesten, Clotrimaderm, and generics)
  • Miconazole 2% cream (e.g., Micatin, Monistat Derm, and generics)
  • Terbinafine 1% cream (e.g., Lamisil AT) — note: terbinafine is highly effective topically but oral terbinafine does not work well for tinea versicolor

Apply to affected areas once or twice daily for 2 to 4 weeks. These creams work well for limited patches but can be impractical to apply over large areas of the back.

A Note on Skin Colour After Treatment

Clearing the yeast does not immediately restore normal skin colour. Hypopigmented (pale) patches in particular can take several months to fade — sometimes longer in people with darker skin tones. This is because the pigment-producing cells (melanocytes) need time to recover and repopulate the affected area. Sun exposure (with appropriate sun protection) can gently help speed repigmentation. Persistence of colour change does not mean the treatment has failed — as long as the scale and active yeast are gone, the treatment has worked.

When Prescription Treatment Is Needed

If over-the-counter treatments have not cleared the condition after a proper trial, or if the affected area is very widespread, your doctor may recommend prescription-strength options.9,10

Oral Antifungals and Drug Interactions

Oral antifungal medications (particularly itraconazole and fluconazole) interact with a number of commonly prescribed drugs, including blood thinners, certain heart and cholesterol medications, and others. Always let your pharmacist and doctor know all medications you are taking before starting an oral antifungal. Topical treatments do not carry this concern.

Preventing Recurrence

Tinea versicolor has a well-known tendency to recur, particularly in people who live in warm climates, sweat heavily, or have naturally oily skin. Recurrence rates without any preventive strategy are reported at 40 to 60% within one year.3,12 Fortunately, recurrence can be substantially reduced with a simple maintenance approach.

Maintenance Strategy (After Initial Clearance)

Once the active infection has been treated, many people benefit from a monthly or seasonal maintenance wash to suppress regrowth:

  • Apply selenium sulfide or ketoconazole shampoo to the trunk and shoulders once monthly, leave on for 10 minutes, then rinse. This is particularly useful in the spring and summer months in Canada.
  • Some guidelines support prophylactic oral itraconazole (200 mg twice monthly, or as directed by your doctor) for people with frequent recurrences — though most people can manage well with topical prevention alone.12

Other practical measures that may help reduce recurrence include:3,12

  • Wearing breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics during exercise
  • Showering promptly after heavy sweating
  • Avoiding very heavy, occlusive skin products (thick creams or oils) on the trunk
  • Treating recurrences early when you recognize the first signs

When to See Your Doctor

Most cases of tinea versicolor can be self-managed with OTC treatments, but please make an appointment if:

  • Two weeks of OTC treatment has produced no improvement
  • The rash is spreading rapidly or is very widespread (covering much of the trunk or face)
  • You are uncertain about the diagnosis — several other conditions can look similar, including vitiligo, seborrheic dermatitis, and pityriasis rosea
  • The condition recurs frequently and is affecting your quality of life
  • You are pregnant or have significant other health conditions
  • The patches are red, painful, or weeping — which would suggest a secondary bacterial infection

Conditions That Can Look Similar

A few other skin conditions can mimic the appearance of tinea versicolor. Your doctor will consider these if the presentation is atypical or the rash does not respond to treatment:1,5

  • Vitiligo: Produces sharply defined white patches due to permanent destruction of melanocytes, usually without scale. Unlike tinea versicolor, vitiligo is not caused by yeast and does not respond to antifungal treatment.
  • Pityriasis rosea: A self-limiting viral-associated rash producing oval, scaly patches typically arranged in a "Christmas tree" pattern on the trunk, often preceded by a single larger "herald patch."
  • Seborrheic dermatitis: Greasy, yellowish scale on the chest, face, or scalp — also caused by Malassezia but involving a different inflammatory process and requiring somewhat different management.
  • Pityriasis alba: Pale, slightly scaly patches on the face and upper arms — common in children and associated with eczema.
  • Secondary syphilis: Can rarely produce a diffuse scaly truncal rash that may be mistaken for tinea versicolor. Testing is recommended if risk factors are present.

The Bottom Line

Tinea versicolor is a common, harmless, and very treatable condition. It says nothing about your hygiene or health habits — it simply reflects the behaviour of a yeast that everyone carries on their skin. A two-week course of an antifungal shampoo used as a body wash, available at any pharmacy without a prescription, clears the active infection in the majority of people. Once treated, be patient with the colour changes — they will normalize over time. A simple monthly maintenance wash can keep recurrences at bay.
