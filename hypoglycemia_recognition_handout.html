Hypoglycemia Recognition and Management

Prepared by your family doctor
January 2025
What You Need to Know: Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) is defined as blood sugar below 4.0 mmol/L (70 mg/dL). It's a medication side effect, not a diet side effect. Quick recognition and proper treatment can prevent serious complications.

Understanding Hypoglycemia

Hypoglycemia occurs when your blood glucose drops below normal levels, specifically less than 4.0 mmol/L (70 mg/dL). Your brain depends on glucose for energy, so when levels drop too low, it affects your thinking, coordination, and can lead to loss of consciousness if untreated.

Why It Happens on Low-Carb Diets

Important Understanding: Hypoglycemia is not caused by your low-carb diet itself. It happens when diabetes medications continue working as if you're eating carbohydrates, but you're not. This is why we adjust your medications proactively.

When you reduce carbohydrates, certain diabetes medications can drive your blood sugar too low:

  • Sulfonylureas: Gliclazide, glyburide, glimepiride
  • Meglitinides: Repaglinide
  • Insulin: All types, especially mealtime insulin

The Traffic Light Action Plan

🔴RED LIGHT: DANGER

Blood Sugar: Less than 4.0 mmol/L (70 mg/dL)

Immediate Action:

  • Eat 15 grams of fast-acting sugar immediately:
    • 4 glucose tablets
    • 175 mL (3/4 cup) regular juice
    • 15 mL (1 tablespoon) honey
    • 6-8 hard candies
  • Wait 15 minutes, then recheck blood sugar
  • If still below 4.0 mmol/L, repeat treatment

Medication Actions:

  • Next dose: SKIP your next sulfonylurea or mealtime insulin
  • Tonight: Reduce long-acting insulin dose as directed
  • Contact: Call our office within 24 hours

🟡YELLOW LIGHT: CAUTION

Blood Sugar: Greater than 12.0 mmol/L (216 mg/dL)

First Ask Yourself:

Did I eat bread, pasta, rice, or sugar yesterday?

Action Plan:

  • Return to your low-carb diet today
  • Drink plenty of water
  • Do NOT increase insulin immediately
  • Wait 2 days before considering medication changes
  • Remember: "Never fix a diet mistake with a drug mistake"

🟢GREEN LIGHT: SAFE ZONE

Blood Sugar: 5.0-10.0 mmol/L (90-180 mg/dL)

You're Doing Great!

  • Continue following your dietary plan
  • Take medications as prescribed
  • Keep monitoring as directed
  • This is your target range

Recognizing Hypoglycemia Symptoms

Early Warning Signs Severe Symptoms
• Shakiness or trembling
• Sweating
• Rapid heartbeat
• Hunger
• Anxiety or nervousness 		• Confusion
• Difficulty concentrating
• Slurred speech
• Dizziness
• Loss of consciousness
Emergency Alert: If someone with diabetes is unconscious or having a seizure, call 911 immediately. Do NOT try to give food or drinks to an unconscious person.

Prevention Strategies

Daily Prevention Checklist

  • Check blood sugar before meals and bedtime
  • Take medications exactly as prescribed
  • Eat meals at regular times
  • Carry glucose tablets or fast-acting sugar
  • Wear medical identification
  • Know your medication names and doses
  • Keep a blood sugar log

Special Situations

Exercise: Check blood sugar before, during, and after exercise. You may need to eat a small snack or reduce insulin doses. Discuss exercise plans with your healthcare team.
Alcohol: Alcohol can cause delayed hypoglycemia, especially on an empty stomach. If you drink alcohol, eat food with it and check blood sugar more frequently for 24 hours.

For Other Healthcare Providers

Please show this message to other healthcare professionals if you seek external care:

"I am under the supervision of my physician for a therapeutic carbohydrate reduction trial. We have intentionally reduced my insulin/sulfonylurea doses to prevent hypoglycemia while I adapt to this diet. Please do not increase my medication doses without assessing my dietary compliance first. If my blood sugars are elevated, it is likely because I consumed carbohydrates, not because I need more medication."

When to Contact Your Healthcare Team

Call Within 24 Hours If:
  • You experience any hypoglycemia episode
  • Blood sugars consistently run above 15.0 mmol/L
  • You're unsure about medication adjustments
  • You have questions about symptom management

Call 911 Immediately If:

• Loss of consciousness
• Seizure
• Severe confusion or inability to swallow
• Blood sugar below 2.8 mmol/L (50 mg/dL)

Our "Golden Rule" During Transition

"Run Them High" - During your transition to low-carb eating, it's medically safer for your blood sugar to run slightly higher for a few weeks while you adapt than to experience severe hypoglycemia. We can always fine-tune your medications once you're stable on your new eating pattern.
