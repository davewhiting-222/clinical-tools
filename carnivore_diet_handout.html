The Carnivore Diet

A Balanced Look at the Evidence — What It Is, What the Research Shows, and Who It May (or May Not) Be Right For

Why This Topic Matters — and Why It's So Contentious

Few dietary topics generate more heat in medical circles than the carnivore diet. Enthusiastic proponents credit it with reversing autoimmune disease, treatment-resistant depression, and obesity. Mainstream nutritional medicine views it with deep concern, citing decades of evidence linking plant-rich diets to longevity and cancer prevention. This handout does not take a side. It tries to give you an honest account of what the current science actually shows — including its limitations — so you can have an informed conversation with your doctor.

What Is the Carnivore Diet?

The carnivore diet consists exclusively of animal-based foods: meat, fish, eggs, and (in most versions) some dairy such as butter and hard cheese. All plant foods are eliminated — including vegetables, fruit, grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, and any processed plant-derived ingredients. There is no fibre, no plant polyphenols, and essentially no carbohydrates. Because fat and protein are the only macronutrients, the body shifts into a state of nutritional ketosis — burning fat as its primary fuel, similar to the well-studied ketogenic diet, but taken considerably further.1,2

Proponents often describe it as the logical extreme of low-carbohydrate eating, while critics view it as the most nutritionally radical mainstream diet to have emerged in recent decades. Both are largely correct. It is a genuinely extreme intervention — which means its potential benefits and risks are correspondingly amplified.1

The Carnivore Diet: From Low-Carb to Elimination

The carnivore diet sits at the far end of the animal-food spectrum. Most low-carbohydrate diets retain vegetables and some plant foods; the carnivore diet removes all of them.

Who Is Trying It and Why?

People turn to the carnivore diet for a wide range of reasons. The most commonly reported motivations include:1,2,3

  • Autoimmune or inflammatory conditions that have not responded to conventional treatment (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis)
  • Persistent gut symptoms, including bloating, pain, and diarrhoea — sometimes labelled as IBS — that improve when plant foods are eliminated
  • Obesity and metabolic syndrome — particularly when other diets have failed or where insulin resistance is the central problem
  • Treatment-resistant depression, anxiety, or other mental health conditions
  • Chronic fatigue or unexplained multi-system symptoms

A common thread is that many adopters have tried standard dietary advice without meaningful benefit, and have turned to the carnivore diet as a radical elimination protocol — essentially a last resort. This context matters greatly when interpreting reports of dramatic improvement.

The Strongest Evidence in Favour

1. Metabolic Health and Blood Sugar

The most robust evidence for very low-carbohydrate eating comes from the field of metabolic medicine. Because the carnivore diet contains essentially zero carbohydrates, it produces profound reductions in blood glucose and insulin levels — more so than any other dietary approach. Dr. Eric Westman at Duke University has published extensively on low-carbohydrate diets for type 2 diabetes, demonstrating that eliminating carbohydrates is one of the most powerful interventions available for lowering blood sugar and reducing insulin requirements.4 The carnivore diet takes this principle to its logical extreme.

Metabolic Improvements: What Studies Show

A 2025 nutrient composition analysis found that carnivore diets can meet most macronutrient needs and are associated with significant improvements in glycaemic markers, weight, and self-reported wellbeing in adherents.3 These findings are consistent with the well-established metabolic benefits of ketogenic eating — reduced insulin demand, improved triglycerides, and increased HDL cholesterol — all of which are relevant to cardiovascular risk reduction in the context of insulin resistance.

2. Mental Health: An Emerging Signal Worth Taking Seriously

One of the most surprising and clinically interesting areas of carnivore/ketogenic research involves mental health. A 2022 retrospective analysis by Danan and colleagues — including Drs. Westman and Georgia Ede — examined 31 hospitalised patients with serious, treatment-resistant psychiatric illness (major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, schizoaffective disorder) who were placed on a ketogenic diet of no more than 20 grams of carbohydrate per day. Among the 28 patients who adhered to the diet for more than two weeks, all showed clinically meaningful improvement in depression and psychosis scores. Mean depression ratings (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) fell from 25.4 to 7.7, a reduction of nearly 70%.5

Important Limitations of the Mental Health Data

The Danan 2022 study was a retrospective, uncontrolled analysis — there was no placebo group, no randomisation, and it was conducted in a supervised hospital setting where many other factors (structured routine, reduced alcohol, improved sleep) may have contributed to improvement. The results are striking and hypothesis-generating, but they do not yet establish ketogenic or carnivore diets as a proven psychiatric treatment. Well-designed randomised controlled trials are now underway at several institutions, and their results will be critical.5,6

3. The Cholesterol Question: A Genuinely Interesting Finding

One of the most contentious aspects of the carnivore and ketogenic diets is their effect on LDL cholesterol ("bad" cholesterol). For many people on very low-carbohydrate diets, LDL levels rise significantly — sometimes to levels that would typically prompt a discussion about statin therapy. This has understandably alarmed clinicians trained to view elevated LDL as a reliable marker of cardiovascular risk.

However, a phenotype has been identified — the "lean mass hyper-responder" (LMHR) — in which metabolically healthy, lean individuals on a ketogenic diet develop very high LDL levels alongside very high HDL and very low triglycerides. Whether this LDL elevation carries the same cardiovascular risk as LDL elevation in a person with metabolic syndrome is genuinely unknown. The KETO-CTA study (published in JACC: Advances, 2025) followed 100 LMHRs over one year using coronary CT angiography and found that elevated LDL and ApoB levels were not associated with plaque progression in this metabolically healthy population. Baseline plaque — not LDL — was the strongest predictor of plaque progression.7

What the KETO-CTA Study Does — and Does Not — Tell Us

The KETO-CTA study is important, but it has significant limitations that have been acknowledged by both its authors and independent reviewers. It was small (n=100), uncontrolled, of short duration (one year), and enrolled a highly selected group who were already on a ketogenic diet and metabolically healthy. It cannot be generalised to people who are overweight, metabolically unwell, or who develop LDL elevations in the context of a less healthy metabolic picture. The results are best described as hypothesis-generating, not practice-changing. Decades of evidence linking elevated LDL to cardiovascular disease remain intact and should not be dismissed on the basis of this single study.7,8

4. Gut Adaptation: More Flexible Than We Thought?

A small but intriguing 2024 case study examined the gut microbiome of a healthy 32-year-old male who had followed a strict carnivore diet for four years. Contrary to expectations, the gut microbiome was not depleted or dysbiotic — it was dominated by bacteria typically associated with fibre fermentation, suggesting the gut may adapt to an animal-only diet in at least some individuals. There were no differences in microbial diversity compared to control groups eating mixed diets.9

This is a single case study of one person — it cannot be used to draw population-level conclusions. However, it does raise interesting questions about gut adaptability that deserve more rigorous investigation.

The Strongest Evidence Against

1. Fibre, Gut Health, and Longevity

The carnivore diet contains zero dietary fibre. This is perhaps its most significant nutritional departure from conventional evidence-based eating. A major 2015 meta-analysis of cohort studies found that for every additional 10 grams of fibre consumed per day, all-cause mortality fell by approximately 10%. High fibre intake was also associated with reductions in cardiovascular and cancer mortality.10 A 2019 Lancet systematic review involving data from multiple large populations reported that high-fibre diets were associated with 15–30% reductions in all-cause and cardiovascular mortality compared to low-fibre diets.11

The Fibre Problem: A Real and Unresolved Concern

The association between dietary fibre and reduced mortality is one of the most consistently replicated findings in nutritional epidemiology. It has been observed across diverse populations and study designs. Dismissing it requires strong counter-evidence. The carnivore community argues that these are observational studies — they cannot prove causation, and high-fibre diets are also associated with many other healthy behaviours (confounding). This is a legitimate methodological point. But the consistency and magnitude of the association across dozens of large cohorts is not easily dismissed. This remains the most important unanswered question about the carnivore diet's long-term safety.10,11

2. Colon Cancer Risk

High red and processed meat consumption has been classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC/WHO) as a probable (Group 2A) and definite (Group 1) carcinogen for colorectal cancer, respectively. While the carnivore community correctly points out that these associations are largely observational and that red meat in the context of an otherwise healthy diet may carry different risk than red meat consumed alongside processed foods and refined sugars, this classification is based on a substantial body of evidence and cannot be simply set aside. The long-term colorectal cancer implications of a strictly carnivore diet remain entirely unknown.12

3. Nutrient Adequacy: Some Gaps, Some Surprises

A 2025 nutrient composition analysis of several carnivore diet meal plans found that while the diet can be rich in protein, B vitamins (particularly B12), zinc, iron, and selenium, it is reliably low in vitamin C, vitamin E, folate, calcium (in non-dairy versions), potassium, and magnesium. Some nutrients typically obtained from plants — such as vitamin K1 and certain polyphenols — are absent.3 Whether these gaps cause clinical problems over years or decades in adherents who do not develop scurvy (which requires very low vitamin C intakes sustained over months) is unknown. Organ meats, particularly liver, significantly improve micronutrient adequacy and are consumed by many experienced carnivore practitioners.

4. The "Healthy User" Bias Problem

Much of the negative observational data on red meat comes from studies where heavy meat consumers also tended to smoke more, exercise less, and consume more processed foods alongside their meat. Conversely, much of the anecdotal success reported by carnivore dieters comes from people who have simultaneously eliminated ultra-processed foods, alcohol, sugar, and refined grains — any one of which alone could account for substantial health improvement. Separating the signal of "carnivore diet" from the signal of "elimination of junk food" is currently impossible based on available evidence.1

Comparing the Two Sides: A Summary

Points of Agreement and Genuine Uncertainty

The "Hidden Gem": Why Context Changes Everything

One of the most important insights from the carnivore diet debate is that the same diet may have very different effects in different people, in different metabolic contexts, and over different time periods. Several nuances are critical to understanding the real-world evidence:1,13

The Elimination Principle

Many people who report dramatic improvement on a carnivore diet have spent years struggling with symptoms that conventional medicine has not resolved — chronic gut symptoms, inflammatory conditions, fatigue, or treatment-resistant depression. For these individuals, the carnivore diet functions as a radical elimination protocol, removing all potential dietary triggers simultaneously. Some people who respond to the carnivore diet find that they can later reintroduce certain plant foods without symptoms returning — suggesting the issue was a specific food sensitivity rather than a need to avoid all plants permanently.

The "Oreo vs. Statin" Experiment

Researcher Dr. Nick Norwitz conducted a self-experiment in which he consumed Oreo cookies (high carbohydrate) for a period while on a background ketogenic diet. His LDL cholesterol, which had been elevated on the ketogenic diet, fell more dramatically than it had on a statin medication. While this is a single n=1 experiment with obvious limitations, it illustrates a genuinely important concept: in some metabolically healthy, lean individuals ("lean mass hyper-responders"), LDL appears to be driven by the body's need to transport fat as fuel when running on a ketogenic substrate, and may behave differently from LDL elevation in a metabolically unhealthy context.13 This does not mean LDL elevation on the carnivore diet is harmless for everyone — but it raises legitimate questions about whether standard LDL thresholds apply equally in all metabolic states.

Whole Foods vs. Processed Junk

Much of the observational data linking high red meat intake to poor health comes from populations consuming red meat alongside ultra-processed foods, refined carbohydrates, sugar-sweetened beverages, and a sedentary lifestyle. A person eating steak, eggs, and butter while simultaneously eliminating all processed food, sugar, alcohol, and refined grains is in a nutritionally very different situation from someone eating a fast-food-heavy diet with added red meat. The "health user bias" — where people eating healthier overall tend to also make other healthy choices — complicates the interpretation of meat-specific health data in both directions.1

Who Might Have the Most to Gain?

Based on current (limited) evidence, the populations most likely to benefit from a trial of carnivore or very-low-carbohydrate carnivore-style eating include:1,2,4,5

  • People with type 2 diabetes or severe insulin resistance who have not responded adequately to standard dietary advice — particularly when medication reduction is a goal
  • People with treatment-resistant autoimmune or inflammatory conditions, particularly gut-related (Crohn's disease, severe IBS), who have failed standard treatment
  • People with treatment-resistant mood disorders — particularly in the context of metabolic dysregulation — who are willing to try a structured dietary intervention under medical supervision
  • People with severe food sensitivities who need a rigorous elimination protocol to identify triggers

Who Should Approach with Extra Caution

The carnivore diet carries particular risks in the following groups and should not be attempted without close medical supervision:2,3

  • People on insulin or sulfonylurea medications — blood sugar may drop rapidly and dangerously
  • People with kidney disease — the high protein load may accelerate decline in kidney function
  • People with familial hypercholesterolaemia or established cardiovascular disease — the LDL implications are more concerning in this context
  • People with a history of eating disorders
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women
  • Children and adolescents

What Should You Monitor If You Try It?

If you and your doctor agree that a trial of the carnivore diet is appropriate, close monitoring is essential — particularly in the early weeks when metabolic shifts are most dramatic and medication doses may need adjustment.2,4

🔬 Recommended Lab Monitoring

  • Fasting glucose and HbA1c (3-monthly initially)
  • Full lipid panel including LDL, HDL, triglycerides, and ApoB if available
  • Liver enzymes (ALT/AST)
  • Kidney function (creatinine, eGFR, urine protein)
  • Uric acid (can rise on high-protein diets)
  • Vitamin C, B12, folate, magnesium, ferritin
  • CRP (inflammation marker)

⚠️ Clinical Warning Signs

  • Hypoglycaemia symptoms (dizziness, shaking) if on diabetes medications — seek immediate dose adjustment
  • Worsening kidney function
  • Constipation (common without fibre — ensure adequate hydration and electrolytes)
  • Elevated uric acid / gout flares
  • Severe LDL elevation in someone with cardiovascular disease or familial hypercholesterolaemia
  • Disordered eating patterns or food preoccupation

The Bottom Line: A Genuinely Open Question

The honest answer is that we do not yet know the long-term health consequences of a strictly carnivore diet. No randomised controlled trial has followed people on a carnivore diet for five, ten, or twenty years and measured hard clinical outcomes — cardiovascular events, cancer incidence, mortality. The diet is simply too new as a mainstream phenomenon for this evidence to exist yet.1,2

What we do know is this: in the short to medium term, very-low-carbohydrate and carnivore eating can produce dramatic improvements in metabolic markers — blood sugar, insulin, triglycerides, weight — that are clinically meaningful, particularly for people with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. There are also genuinely intriguing early signals in psychiatric medicine that deserve rigorous follow-up. At the same time, the elimination of all dietary fibre runs directly against one of the most consistently replicated findings in nutritional epidemiology, and this cannot be waved away.

A Reasonable Working Conclusion

The carnivore diet appears to be a powerful therapeutic tool for certain individuals in certain clinical contexts — particularly metabolic disease, severe insulin resistance, and possibly treatment-resistant inflammatory or psychiatric conditions. It is not established as a generally safe long-term dietary pattern for the average person, and the absence of long-term safety data means that anyone following it should do so with medical supervision and regular monitoring. The strongest version of the pro-carnivore argument is not "this is the optimal diet for everyone" but rather "for some people with specific conditions, this extreme intervention may produce improvements that conventional approaches have failed to achieve." That is a much more defensible claim — and one that deserves to be taken seriously.

Having This Conversation With Your Doctor

If you are interested in trying a carnivore or very low-carbohydrate diet, your doctor can help you assess whether the potential benefits outweigh the risks in your specific situation, establish baseline blood work before you start, monitor for early warning signs, adjust medications as your metabolism shifts, and help you evaluate whether the diet is actually working for you based on objective data — not just how you feel. Diet is a powerful tool. Like any powerful tool, it works best when used with guidance and with a clear understanding of both its capabilities and its risks.
