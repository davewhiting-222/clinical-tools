Managing Your Latent TB Infection

Prepared by your family doctor June 24, 2026

What Is Latent TB?

TB stands for tuberculosis — a bacterial infection caused by germs called Mycobacterium tuberculosis. When someone has latent TB, those germs are living quietly inside the body but are not active. This means:

  • You feel completely well.
  • Your chest X-ray and breathing tests are normal — there is no active disease in your lungs.
  • You cannot spread TB to anyone else right now.
🔑 Key Point
Latent TB is not the same as active TB. You are not sick, and you are not contagious. But without treatment, latent TB can "wake up" and become active disease — especially when the immune system (the body's defence system) is under pressure.

Why Your Risk Is a Little Higher

Most healthy adults with latent TB have about a 5–10% lifetime chance of the infection becoming active. For you, that risk is somewhat higher because:

  • Age over 65 — the immune system naturally becomes less robust over time.
  • Diabetes — high blood sugar weakens the immune system's ability to keep the TB germs "asleep."
  • Dialysis (kidney dialysis) — kidney failure and dialysis reduce immune function significantly.
⚠️ Why Treatment Matters
Treating latent TB now — while you feel well — is far safer and easier than treating active TB later. A full course of treatment reduces your risk of the infection becoming active by about 60–90%.1

Your Treatment Plan: Rifampin (Rifampicin) Once Daily for 4 Months

Your doctor has prescribed rifampin (RIF-am-pin) 600 mg — one tablet taken once each day with food for 16 weeks (4 months). This regimen is known as 4R and is one of the preferred short-course options recommended by Canadian and international guidelines for latent TB treatment.2,3

✅ A Practical Advantage
The 4-month rifampin course has been shown in a large clinical trial to be just as effective as the older 9-month treatment, with fewer side effects and better completion rates.4 It is a shorter commitment with a strong track record.

How to Take Your Rifampin

  • Take it at the same time each day — this makes it easier to remember.
  • Take it with food to reduce stomach upset.
  • If you miss a dose, take it as soon as you remember — unless it is almost time for your next dose. Never double up.
  • Do not stop taking it without talking to your doctor first, even if you feel fine.
💊 A Public-Health Nurse Will Support You
You are not alone in this — there is a team supporting you.

Expect an Orange Colour — This Is Normal

Rifampin turns body fluids an orange-red colour. This includes your urine, sweat, tears, and saliva. This is completely harmless but can be surprising. A few things to know:

  • Soft contact lenses can be permanently stained — wear glasses during treatment if possible.
  • The colour goes away once you finish the medication.

Side Effects: What to Watch For

Most people tolerate rifampin well, but it is important to know what symptoms need prompt attention. The table below summarizes the most important side effects.

Side Effect How Common What It Looks Like What to Do
Liver irritation (hepatotoxicity) Uncommon (<5%) Yellowing of the skin or whites of the eyes (jaundice), dark urine, nausea, loss of appetite, upper-right belly pain, unusual tiredness Stop medication and contact us immediately
Stomach upset / nausea Common (up to 10%) Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, stomach cramps Take with food; call us if severe or persistent
Skin reactions / rash Uncommon Itching, hives, red rash Call us; a mild rash may be manageable, a severe rash needs urgent review
Flu-like symptoms Rare with daily dosing Fever, chills, muscle aches, feeling unwell Call us to discuss
Low platelet count Rare Unusual bruising, bleeding gums, blood in urine or stool Stop medication and contact us immediately
Orange-red body fluids Very common (expected) Orange urine, sweat, tears, saliva Normal — no action needed. Avoid soft contact lenses.
Drug interactions Very common Rifampin speeds up liver enzymes that break down many medications, reducing their effectiveness See Drug Interactions section below — important for your situation
🚨 Stop Medication and Seek Care Right Away If You Have:
  • Yellow skin or yellow eyes (jaundice)
  • Dark brown or cola-coloured urine (not the normal orange tint from rifampin)
  • Severe nausea, vomiting, or belly pain you cannot manage
  • Unexplained bruising or unusual bleeding
  • A severe or spreading rash

Call Dr. Whiting's office, your public-health nurse, or go to the emergency department.

Important Drug Interactions

Rifampin is a powerful "enzyme inducer" (it makes your liver work faster at breaking down many medicines). This means some of your other medications may not work as well while you are on rifampin — or their levels in your blood may change in ways that need monitoring. The following is important for your specific situation:

Cholesterol Medicine (Statin) — Paused During Treatment

⚠️ Pause Your Statin While on Rifampin

Your cholesterol-lowering medicine (a "statin") will be paused while you take rifampin. This is because rifampin dramatically lowers the level of statins in your blood, making them much less effective — and in some cases it can increase the risk of muscle side effects. Once you finish the 4-month rifampin course, we will restart your statin. This short break is safe for most people and is standard practice.5

Other Medications to Be Aware Of

Rifampin can interact with many common medicines. Tell any other doctor, pharmacist, or specialist that you are taking rifampin. Important examples include:

  • Blood pressure medications — levels may drop; we will monitor your blood pressure closely.
  • Blood thinners (e.g., warfarin) — dose adjustments are often needed; more frequent INR checks required.
  • Diabetes medications — blood sugar control may change; we may need to adjust your diabetes treatment.
  • Certain heart medications — discuss with your specialist.
  • Oral contraceptives — rifampin makes the birth control pill less effective. Use a backup method (e.g., condoms) for the duration of treatment and one month after.

Blood Tests and Monitoring During Treatment

Because of your age, diabetes, and dialysis, we will monitor your blood closely throughout treatment. Your public-health nurse will co-ordinate these tests with your dialysis team. Here is what to expect:

Test Why We Do It Timing
Liver function tests (LFTs) — AST, ALT, bilirubin, ALP To check for liver irritation from rifampin Before starting, then monthly during treatment
Complete blood count (CBC) To check blood cells — especially platelets (clotting cells) and red blood cells Before starting, then monthly
Kidney function / dialysis labs To monitor ongoing kidney health and dialysis adequacy As per your usual dialysis schedule
Blood pressure Rifampin may lower the level of some blood pressure medicines in the blood At each visit / nurse check-in
Blood sugar (glucose) monitoring Rifampin can affect blood sugar control in people with diabetes Continue your usual monitoring; report changes to us
🔬 Why These Tests Matter
Regular blood tests let us catch any problem early — often before you even notice a symptom. If a test result is concerning, we will adjust your plan right away. Most people complete treatment without any significant issues.

Your Situation: Diabetes and Dialysis

Diabetes

Rifampin can interact with some diabetes medications and may affect your blood sugar control. Check your blood sugar as you normally would, and let us know if you notice your levels are higher or lower than usual. We may need to adjust your diabetes medication during treatment.

Dialysis

Rifampin is not significantly removed by dialysis, so no dose adjustment is needed specifically because of your dialysis. However, your dialysis team will be kept in the loop so everyone is co-ordinating your care together.

Your Action Plan Checklist

Use this checklist to stay on track throughout your treatment:

  • Attend all scheduled blood test appointments arranged by your public-health nurse.
  • Take rifampin 600 mg every day with food at the same time.
  • Do not drink alcohol while on rifampin — it increases the strain on your liver.
  • Pause your statin (cholesterol medicine) as instructed — restart only after discussing with Dr. Whiting.
  • Monitor your blood pressure at home if you have a blood pressure cuff, and record the readings.
  • Continue your usual blood sugar monitoring and report significant changes.
  • Tell all your healthcare providers (dialysis nurses, specialists, pharmacist) that you are taking rifampin.
  • Avoid soft contact lenses for the duration of treatment.
  • Know the warning signs that mean you should stop the medication and call immediately (see red box above).
  • Keep all follow-up appointments with Dr. Whiting and your public-health nurse.
🚫 Alcohol and Rifampin: A Serious Combination
Alcohol and rifampin both put stress on your liver. We strongly recommend avoiding alcohol completely during your 4 months of treatment. This is especially important given your other health conditions.

Finishing Your Treatment

It is crucial to complete all 16 weeks of rifampin, even when you feel perfectly well. Stopping early may mean the TB germs are not fully suppressed and the treatment will not give you the protection you need.

🏁 The Finish Line Is Worth It
Completing your full course of rifampin is one of the most important things you can do for your long-term health. Once treatment is done, you will not need ongoing follow-up specifically for latent TB, and your risk of ever developing active TB disease drops dramatically.
💬 A Word of Encouragement
Being diagnosed with latent TB — and choosing to treat it — is a proactive, health-forward decision. You are taking control of something before it becomes a problem. That takes courage, especially when you are already managing other health challenges. Your care team is with you every step of the way.

When to Contact Dr. Whiting or Your Nurse

  • Any new symptom that worries you after starting rifampin.
  • Yellowing of your skin or eyes, unusual tiredness, loss of appetite, or belly pain.
  • Nausea or vomiting that is making it difficult to take your medication.
  • Unexplained bruising or bleeding.
  • A skin rash that is spreading or bothering you.
  • Blood sugar levels that are much higher or lower than usual.
  • Blood pressure readings that seem very different from your normal range.
  • Any questions about other medications and whether they might be affected.
📞 Contact Information

If you are unsure whether a symptom needs attention, it is always better to call. No question is too small.

If you are having a medical emergency, call 9-1-1 or go to your nearest emergency department.
