Managing Heartburn in Adults

Prepared by your family doctor
December 2024
What You Need to Know: Heartburn is a common condition that affects up to 20% of Canadians. While often manageable with lifestyle changes and over-the-counter medications, it's important to recognize when professional medical evaluation is needed.

Understanding Heartburn

Heartburn is a burning sensation in your chest that occurs when stomach acid backs up into your esophagus (the tube connecting your mouth to your stomach). This happens when the lower esophageal sphincter (a muscle that acts like a valve) doesn't close properly.

Most Common Causes

  • Large meals: Overeating puts extra pressure on your stomach
  • Trigger foods: Spicy foods, citrus fruits, tomatoes, chocolate, coffee, alcohol
  • Lying down after eating: Gravity normally helps keep acid in your stomach
  • Being overweight: Extra weight increases pressure on your stomach
  • Smoking: Weakens the valve between your stomach and esophagus
  • Pregnancy: Hormones and pressure from the growing baby
  • Certain medications: Including some pain relievers and blood pressure medications
  • Stress: Can increase stomach acid production

Lifestyle Changes That Help

Good News: Many people find significant relief through simple lifestyle modifications. These changes work best when used together consistently.

Eating Habits

  • Eat smaller, more frequent meals instead of three large ones
  • Chew your food slowly and thoroughly
  • Stop eating 2-3 hours before bedtime
  • Identify and avoid your personal trigger foods
  • Keep a food diary to track what causes your symptoms

Common Trigger Foods to Limit

Food Category Specific Examples Why It Triggers Heartburn
Acidic Foods Citrus fruits, tomatoes, vinegar Add extra acid to an already acidic environment
Fatty Foods Fried foods, fatty meats, full-fat dairy Slow stomach emptying and relax the valve
Spicy Foods Hot peppers, curry, hot sauce Irritate the esophagus lining
Beverages Coffee, alcohol, carbonated drinks Relax the valve or increase acid production

Physical and Lifestyle Changes

  • Maintain a healthy weight: Even losing 5-10 pounds can make a difference
  • Elevate your head when sleeping: Use a wedge pillow or raise the head of your bed 15-20 cm
  • Wear loose-fitting clothes: Tight clothing can put pressure on your stomach
  • Quit smoking: Smoking weakens the valve and increases acid production
  • Manage stress: Try relaxation techniques, regular exercise, or counselling

Over-the-Counter Medications

Several types of medications are available without a prescription in Canada. Each works differently and has specific uses:

Antacids

  • Examples: Tums, Rolaids, Maalox
  • How they work: Neutralize stomach acid that's already there
  • Best for: Quick relief of occasional heartburn
  • Timing: Take 1-2 hours after meals and at bedtime
  • Caution: Don't use daily for more than 2 weeks without seeing your doctor

H2 Receptor Blockers

  • Examples: Zantac (ranitidine), Pepcid (famotidine)
  • How they work: Reduce the amount of acid your stomach makes
  • Best for: Preventing heartburn before meals or at bedtime
  • Timing: Take 30-60 minutes before eating or before bed
  • Duration: Effects last 6-12 hours

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

  • Examples: Nexium (esomeprazole), Prevacid (lansoprazole)
  • How they work: Block acid production more completely than H2 blockers
  • Best for: Frequent heartburn (2+ days per week)
  • Timing: Take 30-60 minutes before your first meal
  • Important: Take the full course (usually 14 days) even if symptoms improve
Medication Safety: Always read labels carefully and follow dosing instructions. Some medications can interact with other drugs or aren't suitable for everyone. Check with your pharmacist if you're unsure.

When Prescription Medication is Needed

Your doctor may prescribe stronger medications if:

  • Over-the-counter treatments don't provide adequate relief after 2 weeks
  • You have heartburn more than twice a week
  • Your symptoms interfere with daily activities or sleep
  • You have additional symptoms like difficulty swallowing or persistent cough

Prescription Options

  • Higher-dose PPIs: Stronger versions of over-the-counter medications
  • Prokinetics: Help your stomach empty faster (less commonly used)
  • Combination therapy: Sometimes two different types of medications work better together

Red Flags: When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

Seek Emergency Care If You Experience:
  • Severe chest pain, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, or pain radiating to your arm or jaw (could be a heart attack)
  • Severe abdominal pain
  • Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds
  • Black, tarry, or bloody stools
  • Sudden, severe difficulty swallowing

When to Schedule Testing with Your Doctor

You should see your family doctor for further evaluation if you have:

  • Persistent symptoms: Heartburn that doesn't improve with lifestyle changes and over-the-counter medications after 4-6 weeks
  • Frequent heartburn: Symptoms more than twice a week
  • Difficulty swallowing: Food feels stuck or you have pain when swallowing
  • Unintended weight loss: Losing weight without trying
  • Persistent cough or hoarseness: Especially if it's worse at night or in the morning
  • Nausea and vomiting: Ongoing stomach upset
  • Family history: Close relatives with stomach or esophageal cancer
  • Age over 50: With new or worsening symptoms

Possible Tests Your Doctor Might Recommend

  • Upper endoscopy: A thin, flexible tube with a camera to look at your esophagus and stomach
  • Upper GI series (barium swallow): X-rays taken after drinking a contrast solution
  • pH monitoring: Measures acid levels in your esophagus over 24 hours
  • Esophageal manometry: Tests how well the muscles in your esophagus work
